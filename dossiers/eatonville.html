1. Founding

Eatonville stands six miles north of Orlando, in what was the citrus country of Orange County. After Emancipation, formerly enslaved people migrated into central Florida and found work clearing land, planting citrus groves, and building the houses, hotels, and railroad of the new town of Maitland (Town of Eatonville official history). Some of them rose quickly: the Maitland town marshal was Joe Clarke, a Black man, and Black voters were part of Maitland's civic life from its start (Town of Eatonville official history; Florida Backroads Travel summary of the town record).

The town exists because those settlers wanted a government of their own, and because two white men would sell them land when others would not. Joseph E. Clarke and several friends first tried to buy a block of land for what Clarke himself called a "colony for colored people," and, in Clarke's own words as carried in the town's history, "so great was the prejudice then existing against the Negro that no one would sell them land for such a purpose." In 1881, Lewis Lawrence, originally from Utica, New York, agreed to help. Lawrence persuaded his neighbor and friend Captain Josiah C. Eaton, a former Union officer and Maitland landowner, to sell him 22 acres, which Lawrence subdivided into 48 lots priced from $19 to $30 and offered only to Black buyers (Town of Eatonville official history, quoting the Tallahassee Weekly Floridian, 1881). Lawrence had a framed church built on the land, and the deeds carried a temperance covenant: no liquor was ever to be sold or given away on the ground, and no gambling or disreputable house allowed, under forfeiture of the purchase (Weekly Floridian, 1881, as quoted in the town history). The St. Lawrence African Methodist Episcopal Church, founded in 1881 on the settlement's first land, is counted the first African American church in the area, and it still stands and serves the town (BlackPast).

The settlement was at first called Lawrence, after its founder on the ground. In 1884, the Orange County Reporter described the "colored village at Maitland sometimes known as Lawrence, the name of its worthy founder," and Lawrence himself requested that the settlement take the name Eatonville, in honor of Eaton (Town of Eatonville official history). The push for a separate town hardened after Maitland incorporated in 1885: Maitland's incorporation used Black votes, and at least one Black man was elected a town officer, yet within a year there were formal "discussions of separation" as a mutually beneficial answer to the "race problem" (Town of Eatonville official history). Clarke kept buying. One account dates an initial 12-acre purchase by Clarke to November 18, 1885, with his holdings growing over time (James Madison Institute history, via MadameNoire), and by incorporation the town comprised 112 acres (Town of Eatonville official history; BlackPast).

On August 15, 1887, 27 registered Black electors gathered at the town hall and voted to incorporate the Town of Eatonville. They elected the Reverend Columbus H. Boger as the town's first mayor, and Joseph Clarke, Matthew Brazell, David Yelder, E. L. Horn, and E. J. Shines as aldermen, with an entirely Black slate of town officers (Town of Eatonville official history). The claim made for that day needs care, and this dossier makes it carefully: Brooklyn, Illinois had incorporated in 1873 and Princeville, North Carolina was chartered in 1885, so Eatonville was not the first Black town to incorporate in the United States. What Eatonville's own record and most modern accounts support is narrower and still remarkable: it is among the first self-governing all-Black municipalities in the nation, it was incorporated by Black citizens themselves, and it has remained incorporated and self-governing ever since, which is the basis of its standing description as the oldest incorporated African American municipality in America (Town of Eatonville; Association to Preserve the Eatonville Community; see conflict 1).

2. Building the Town

Government came first, and it never left Black hands. The mayor-council government seated in 1887 has run the town continuously. The documented mayoral line runs from Boger (1887-1888) through Clarke (1889-1890, and again 1900-1912), John Hurston (1897-1899, and again 1912-1916), Matthew B. Brazell (1916-1920), and Columbus H. Crooms (1938-1963), down to the present council (mayors list compiled from Florida's Black Public Officials, 1867-1924, and later records, as carried in the town's reference summary). A 1907 photograph of the Eatonville city council and jail, a copy of which is held in the New York Public Library's digital collections, shows the young municipality's government at work.

The town's three founding institutions, in the town history's own framing, were the church, the school, and the family. St. Lawrence A.M.E. dated to 1881, before the town itself (BlackPast). Macedonia Missionary Baptist Church became the town's largest congregation; in 1902 the Reverend John Hurston, Zora Neale Hurston's father, was called as its minister (Hurston biographies based on Robert Hemenway's research). A post office opened in 1889 and closed in 1918 (U.S. Postal Service postmaster records, as cited in the town's reference summary).

The school was the Hungerford. The Robert Hungerford Normal and Industrial School was Eatonville's boarding school for Black students, modeled on Booker T. Washington's Tuskegee Institute and run by Tuskegee graduates Russell C. Calhoun and his wife Mary (Eatonville Master Plan, 2025; ASALH conference scholarship, 2023). The school was named for Dr. Robert Hungerford, a white physician in Maitland who had taught local Black men to read and write; after his death, his family gave land for the school (Eatonville Master Plan). The sources disagree on the founding year, giving 1889, 1897, and 1899 in different accounts; the disagreement is recorded as conflict 5 and is not resolved here. What is documented is the school's scale and reach: more than 100 boarding students by 1927, day students from Eatonville, night classes for adults, a curriculum running from English, Latin, and algebra to industrial arts, agriculture, bookkeeping, and home economics, a campus with dormitories, a chapel, a dining hall, a dairy, and gardens, and cash donations from across the country, including $400 from Booker T. Washington himself (town reference summary of the school's record, citing Clyde W. Hall, 2005).

The town also had a press and a pitch. The Eatonville Speaker, the town newspaper, printed an invitation in 1889 that doubles as the town's founding advertisement: "Colored People of the United States! Solve the Great Race Problem by Securing a Home in Eatonville, Florida, a Negro City Governed by Negroes." The same article described a thriving community of 200 to 300 people, all colored, with not a white family in the whole city, and it recounted a near-lynching in Sanford, nineteen miles away, in the same breath (Eatonville Speaker, 1889, as quoted in the town history). Lots in the town's own advertising went for $35 in cash or $50 on time (Florida Trend, "The Town That Freedom Built"). The first mayor, Boger, also edited a newspaper, the Florida Watchman (Florida's Black Public Officials, 1867-1924).

The economy underneath was citrus and craft. The settlers had come as grove labor and builders; Clarke himself farmed, and a period photograph shows J. E. Clark's pineapple farm in Eatonville. The census arc shows how small the town stayed for how long: 125 people in 1900, 108 in 1910, 125 in 1920, 136 in 1930, then 857 in 1960 and 2,024 in 1970, as the Orlando area grew around it (U.S. Decennial Census; the sourced table skips 1940 and 1950, a gap flagged in the Research log).

3. Peak Years

Eatonville's peak is cultural before it is demographic, and its name is Zora Neale Hurston. She was born on January 7, 1891, in Notasulga, Alabama, the fifth of eight children of John Hurston, a Baptist preacher and carpenter, and Lucy Ann Hurston, a schoolteacher. The family arrived in Eatonville in 1894, when Zora was three (Hurston biographies based on Hemenway). She grew up in a town where her father was mayor (elected 1897) and later the minister of its largest church, where every officer of the law and every teacher she knew was Black, and she said so for the rest of her life: she called Eatonville "home," sometimes claimed it as her birthplace outright, and described growing up there in her 1928 essay "How It Feels to Be Colored Me" (Florida Trend; Hurston biographies; see conflict 8 on her birth claims).

The town she carried into print is documented book by book. Mules and Men (1935), her collection of African American folklore, drew on the tales and tellers of Eatonville (Florida History Network, Florida Memory feature). Their Eyes Were Watching God (1937), her best-known novel, opens and closes in Eatonville and gives the town's founding generation, including its portrait of a self-made Black mayor, to literature (Florida History Network). Her autobiography, Dust Tracks on a Road (1942), tells the Eatonville childhood directly, and she described the town for the Depression-era Federal Writers' Project's Florida guide (Florida History Network). Artist Jules Andre Smith painted Eatonville life in the 1930s and 1940s; twelve of those works are held by the Maitland Art Center in the adjacent town (town reference summary).

The institutions peaked with her. The Hungerford campus grew to some 300 acres by 1950, almost 40 percent of the town's land, held in trust for the school (town reference summary of the land record, citing CBS News reporting, 2023). Club Eaton, on the town's main street, was a stop on the Chitlin' Circuit from at least 1950, hosting performers including B. B. King, Aretha Franklin, Ray Charles, Sam Cooke, the Platters, Duke Ellington, Ella Fitzgerald, Billie Holiday, and James Brown (Eatonville Master Plan, 2025; town reference summary, citing the Orlando Sentinel, August 13, 1987).

The demographic peak came much later and much quieter: 2,432 people in the 2000 census, the high point of the decennial series (U.S. Decennial Census).

4. The Turning Point

Eatonville was never destroyed, and its turning points are fights over land and road rather than a single catastrophe. There are three, and the dossier keeps them separate.

The first was slow: the loss of the Hungerford. The school had been privately funded for half a century; in 1950-1951 the Orange County Public Schools took control and purchased the campus land from its trust for about $16,000, with the stipulation that it be used for the education of Black children (town reference summary of the land record; CBS News, 2023; see conflict 7 on the 1950 versus 1951 dating). The school ran on for decades as a county institution, finally as Robert Hungerford Preparatory High School, Orange County's first all-magnet school of choice, and closed in 2009 (Central Florida Public Media, January 2026; Orlando Sentinel history of the school; see conflict 7 on a source giving 2010). The empty buildings were demolished by the school district during the COVID pandemic (Central Florida Public Media, 2026). What remained was the land: roughly 100 acres of the original campus, appraised in 2019 at $20 million, sitting at the town's gateway (town reference summary of the land record).

The second turning point was a road. In the late 1980s, Orange County proposed widening Kennedy Boulevard, the town's two-lane main street, into a five-lane highway through the middle of Eatonville. Residents organized the Association to Preserve the Eatonville Community (P.E.C.) in 1987 to fight it, under the leadership of N. Y. Nathiri, on the argument that the road would bust the community apart (UCF Public History; Florida Historical Society, Florida Frontiers). The widening was cancelled. The fight's second product outlasted it: as a public-awareness campaign, P.E.C. presented the first Zora Neale Hurston Festival of the Arts and Humanities in 1990, drawing some 10,000 people to the little town, with Alice Walker, who had done more than anyone to restore Hurston's reputation, as the inaugural banquet speaker (Florida Historical Society; Color Magazine festival history).

The third turning point is economic thinness, documented rather than dramatic. As of 2023, Eatonville had no gas station, no supermarket, and no pharmacy, only a Family Dollar, and a median household income of about $27,000 (CBS News Sunday Morning, March 19, 2023). Interstate 4 passes through the town limits without an exit (town reference summary). The town that freedom built has kept its government and its name for almost 140 years while the wealth of metropolitan Orlando accumulated on every side of it.

5. Survival, Decline, or Destruction

Eatonville survived, and the record of its survival is specific.

  • 1970s: Alice Walker locates Hurston's unmarked grave in Fort Pierce, where Hurston had died in poverty in 1960, and buys a headstone for it; her essay "In Search of Zora Neale Hurston" (Ms. magazine, March 1975) sets off the Hurston revival that eventually remakes the town's public standing (Hurston scholarship as carried in the festival and SPLC accounts).
  • 1987: P.E.C. organized to fight the Kennedy Boulevard widening; the road is stopped (UCF Public History; Florida Historical Society).
  • 1990: two foundings. The first ZORA! Festival is presented by P.E.C., and the Zora Neale Hurston National Museum of Fine Arts opens on Kennedy Boulevard (Florida Trend; Eatonville Master Plan).
  • February 3, 1998: the Eatonville Historic District is added to the National Register of Historic Places: 48 historic buildings in a district bounded by Wymore Road, Eaton Street, Fords and East Avenues, Ruffel and Clark Streets (National Register listing data, NPS).
  • 2009: Hungerford Preparatory High School closes (Central Florida Public Media; see conflict 7).
  • 2016: Mayor Anthony Grant, who had served 1994-2009 and returned in 2015, is removed from office after a voter fraud conviction, one episode in a decade of town governance turmoil (West Orlando News; town mayors list).
  • May 1, 2024: the National Trust for Historic Preservation names Eatonville one of America's 11 Most Endangered Historic Places, citing development pressure that threatens to displace the town's residents (National Trust listing, as reported by the Southern Poverty Law Center, May 1, 2024).
  • 2025: a state task force scores Eatonville a close second to St. Augustine as the site for Florida's first Black history museum, about 95 points to St. Augustine's 96 (WESH 2 reporting, via MadameNoire).

The Hungerford land fight, in full, because it is the town's central modern story:

  • February 7, 2023: the Eatonville Town Council votes to reject the comprehensive plan amendments and zoning changes a private developer needed to build on the Hungerford property (P.E.C. v. School Board of Orange County complaint, Case No. 2023-CA-005295-O).
  • March 24, 2023: P.E.C. sues the Orange County School Board, challenging the sale process and arguing that a 1951 deed restriction requiring the land to be used for educational purposes remains in effect (P.E.C. complaint).
  • March 31, 2023: the developer terminates its purchase contract; the first land deal falls through (P.E.C. complaint; Central Florida Public Media).
  • December 31, 2024: the court grants the School Board judgment on the pleadings, reasoning that with no pending sale there is no active case or controversy; P.E.C.'s lawsuit ends (P.E.C. complaint record; Central Florida Public Media describes the suit as thrown out in 2025, a dating variance noted in the complaint's own chronology).
  • September 30, 2025: the School Board declares the property surplus and approves a memorandum of understanding with Dr. Phillips Charities, with Mayor Angie Gardner's support; part of the land is to be donated to the town for a new town center and town history center, with an advisory council of residents and council members (Central Florida Public Media, September 30, 2025; GrowthSpotter, September 23, 2025).
  • January 13, 2026: the School Board votes unanimously to sell the property, now described as about 117 acres in two parcels, to Dr. Phillips Charities. The price is $14 million, with $1 million due upfront and up to $13 million forgiven as milestones are met: $5 million for a community greenspace and pavilion donated to the town, $5 million for an early learning center, $2.5 million for a community hub and health care facility, and $500,000 for a new town hall and history museum. The vote goes ahead despite dissent from the Eatonville Town Council and some residents (Central Florida Public Media, January 13, 2026; Fox 35 Orlando). Whether the sale has closed since the vote is not verified in this pass; see the Research log.

6. The Town Today

A visitor today finds a small, working, majority-Black town of 2,349 people (2020 U.S. Census) on about 1.1 square miles, six miles north of downtown Orlando, still governed from its own town hall at 307 Kennedy Boulevard (Town of Eatonville; project outreach plan). The town's current mayor is Ruth "Ruthi" Critton, per the town's municipal listings as carried in current references (accessed October 2026).

On Kennedy Boulevard, the main street the county once tried to widen: the Zora Neale Hurston National Museum of Fine Arts (opened 1990), exhibiting artists of African descent; the Moseley House Museum, built in 1888 and the town's second-oldest structure, the home of Jim and Matilda Clark Moseley: Matilda, "Miss Tillie," was Hurston's best friend, and the house, restored by 2000 and maintained by the Zeta Educational Thespian Association and Zeta Phi Beta Sorority, shows early Eatonville memorabilia (Eatonville Master Plan, 2025). A library bearing Hurston's name stands on the boulevard as well (opened January 2004 per the town's reference summary, 2006 per Florida History Network; see conflict 10). St. Lawrence A.M.E. Church, founded 1881, still serves the community (BlackPast), as does Macedonia Missionary Baptist, John Hurston's church. The 48-building Eatonville Historic District (National Register, 1998) holds the town's surviving fabric. The Hungerford campus is gone; its roughly 117 acres are vacant land awaiting the Dr. Phillips Charities development described in Section 5.

The keeping organizations, with their roles: the Town of Eatonville itself (townofeatonville.org), the Association to Preserve the Eatonville Community (P.E.C.), which presents the ZORA! Festival and has led the preservation fight since 1987, the Zora Neale Hurston National Museum of Fine Arts, and the Eatonville Historic Preservation Board. The annual ZORA! Festival of the Arts and Humanities, presented by P.E.C. every winter since 1990, is the town's signature event; the 37th annual festival ran January 30 to February 1, 2026 (project outreach plan; zorafestival.org). At its height the festival has drawn crowds reported as high as 100,000 across its events (Florida Trend).

The honest summary for a visitor: the government Zora's father served still sits, the churches still worship, the festival fills the town every winter, and the town's biggest piece of land, the school ground its children were educated on for more than a century, has just been sold out from under the school board fight of 2023-2026 into a charitable development whose promises (a town center, a history museum, an early learning center, affordable housing) are written into the price. Whether those promises are kept is the next chapter, and it is not yet written.

Lyric annotations, line by line

Lyric annotation: written 2026-10-26 from the archived lyrics (suno-lyrics-eatonville-2026-10-26.md), the live Suno display transcribed in the browser pass. Transcription caveat first, because it conditions every quote below: Suno's display of this song contains overlapping-vocal and stutter artifacts, doubled fragments interleaved into the sung text, concentrated in the hooks and parts of Verse 2. The archived text is the best reading of the display, with the artifact spots marked in place; this annotation follows that reading.

  • Verse 1, the founding lines. "In eighteen eighty-seven, freedom struck a chord" matches the incorporation year (August 15, 1887; Key Facts Box). "Twenty-seven men bought land they could afford" is a compression, and the record should be stated plainly beside it: the 27 were registered Black electors who voted to incorporate the town on August 15, 1887 (Section 1). The land story underneath is different in shape: Lewis Lawrence's purchase of 22 acres from Josiah C. Eaton in 1881, subdivided into 48 lots offered only to Black buyers, Joseph Clarke's own purchases on top of that, and a town that comprised 112 acres by incorporation. The line merges the vote and the purchases into one act by one group; as a pointer to who founded the town it aims the right way, and as a statement of what the 27 did it is not the record.
  • Verse 1, the claim line. "The first Black-incorporated town to bear its name" is the overclaim, flagged here as such: it states in the song's strongest form the claim that conflict 1 narrows. Brooklyn, Illinois incorporated in 1873 and Princeville, North Carolina was chartered in 1885, so Eatonville was not the first Black town to incorporate in the United States. The supported claim, the one this dossier carries, is the narrower one: among the first self-governing all-Black municipalities, incorporated by Black citizens themselves, and continuously incorporated since 1887. The site blurb's hedged wording ("one of the first") is correct; the song line is not, and any curriculum use of the lyric should teach the difference, which is exactly the kind of gap the dossier exists to document.
  • Verse 1, the setting lines. "Founded near Orlando where pine trees sway" is supported (six miles north of Orlando; Section 1). "Self-rule, Black schools, and pride in the air" is supported in substance: self-government from 1887 and the Hungerford school are Sections 1 and 2. "While Jim Crow watched, but couldn't reach there" is the verse's one flourish against the record: the town governed itself, and Jim Crow law still surrounded it, close enough that the town's own 1889 newspaper recounted a near-lynching in Sanford, nineteen miles away, in the same breath as its settlement invitation (Section 2). Read it as defiance, not as a factual claim.
  • The Hook. "Eat-on-ville! Built by will! / Free and Black! Ain't goin' back! / Eat-on-ville! Roots so real! / Town on fire! Still inspires!" No checkable factual claim. It is the display's most artifact-heavy passage, and the archived text carries the doubled-fragment example with it.
  • Verse 2, the Hurston couplet. "Where Zora Neale learned stories told by the flame" and "She carved folklore deep with her grandmother's name" are atmospheric, not factual: they make no checkable claim, and nothing in them should be cited as biography. The documented Hurston record lives in the dossier body: born January 7, 1891, in Notasulga, Alabama, brought to Eatonville in 1894 at age three, daughter of a mayor of the town and later minister of its largest church, and the writer who drew Mules and Men (1935) from the tales and tellers of Eatonville and set Their Eyes Were Watching God (1937) in the town (Section 3; her own habit of claiming Eatonville as her birthplace is conflict 8). The couplet's picture of a girl absorbing the town's oral tradition is true to that record in spirit; its details are the song's, not the archive's.
  • Verse 2, the institutions. "Moseley House anchored memories bold" is supported: the Moseley House, built in 1888, is the town's second-oldest structure, the home of Jim and Matilda Clark Moseley, and today the Moseley House Museum holding early Eatonville memorabilia (Section 6). The display artifacts cluster around this stretch of the verse (an artifact marker sits on this line), but the reading is not in doubt. "While the Hungerford School taught hearts of gold" is supported in substance: the Robert Hungerford Normal and Industrial School educated Eatonville's Black students, boarders and day students and adults in night classes, for generations (Sections 2 and 4). The lyric claims no date for the school, so the unresolved founding year (1889, 1897, or 1899; conflict 5) is not engaged by the song.
  • Verse 2, the close. "They danced through decades, segregation be damned" sits on a documented floor: Club Eaton was a Chitlin' Circuit stop from at least 1950 (Section 3). "Black-owned business, Black-led plans" restates the town's founding condition. The remaining lines, including the ones that open "Not just survival" and "A Black blueprint showing freedom alive," are evaluation, not claims; Section 5 documents the survival in detail, and Section 4 documents the economic thinness that keeps the evaluation honest.
  • The Bridge. "They ain't just build a town, they built a future. / Brick by brick, soul by soul. / This ain't forgotten land / It's legacy made whole" (the dash the display puts after "land" is omitted in this quotation; the archive carries the line verbatim). No factual claims. Its vocabulary is the town's own: Eatonville's motto is "The Town That Freedom Built" (Header), and the bridge is a spoken-word restatement of it.
  • The Final Hook. New words, no new claims: "Truth we spill! / History's light! Still in sight! / Born with skill! / Free and Black! That's the fact!"
  • What the shipped lyric avoided. The earlier draft in the same ChatGPT conversation, "Town That Freedom Echoed," carried errors the shipped lyric does not repeat: a founding built on "twenty-two acres" (Lawrence's purchase mistaken for the incorporation itself) and Hungerford closing in "twenty-ten" against the dossier's carried 2009. Those errors never shipped. The shipped lyric's own record problems are the two named above: the first-town overclaim and the twenty-seven-men compression.

Conflicts between sources, recorded not smoothed

  1. The "first" claim. The project master list calls Eatonville the "first incorporated Black municipality in the U.S.," and the town is widely billed as "the first and oldest African American town in the United States" (Face2Face Africa). The dates say otherwise in the broad form: Brooklyn, Illinois incorporated on July 8, 1873, and is the oldest incorporated Black town in the U.S. (town reference summary), and Princeville, North Carolina was chartered by freedmen in 1885, two years before Eatonville (project master list, North Carolina section, which itself calls Princeville the "first town incorporated by freedmen in the U.S."). Eatonville's supported distinction is the narrower one: among the first self-governing all-Black municipalities, incorporated by Black citizens themselves, and continuously incorporated since 1887, the basis for the standard description "oldest incorporated African American municipality in America" (Florida History Network; MadameNoire). The site blurb's hedged wording, "one of the first," is the correct form and is what this dossier carries.
  2. Incorporation date. August 15, 1887 (Town of Eatonville official history; BlackPast; the town's reference summary, which notes that sources disagree on the exact date and points to the town's detailed account) versus August 18, 1887 (West Orlando News). August 15 carried.
  3. First mayor. Columbus H. Boger, 1887-1888 (Town of Eatonville official history; BlackPast; mayors list) versus Joseph Clarke named as the town's first mayor in the James Madison Institute account quoted by MadameNoire. The same MadameNoire item's own history section calls Clarke the "visionary founder and second mayor," matching the town record. Boger carried as first mayor; Clarke as founder and second mayor (and again 1900-1912).
  4. Clarke's name. Joseph E. Clarke (town municipal tradition; mayors list; Face2Face Africa; MadameNoire) versus Joseph C. Clarke (BlackPast), and the spelling "J. E. Clark" in the town reference summary's history text and its caption for his pineapple farm photograph. Carried as Joseph E. Clarke, the form the town and Hurston use; the middle initial and the Clark/Clarke spelling are recorded as variants, not resolved.
  5. Hungerford School founding year. 1889 (Orlando Sentinel history box; Eatonville Master Plan) versus 1897 (town reference summary, citing Clyde W. Hall, 2005) versus 1899 (ASALH 2023 scholarship on Russell and Mary Calhoun, dating their tenure 1899-1920). A reconciliation exists in outline (an 1889 start, with the Calhouns' Hungerford school proper founded a decade later), but no source in this pass states it, and it is not assumed. Unresolved.
  6. The Hungerford gift. The town reference summary says E. C. Hungerford of Chester, Connecticut donated the land (160 acres) in memory of his physician son Robert, who died of yellow fever, and that the campus later grew to 300 acres. The Eatonville Master Plan says Robert Hungerford died of typhoid fever in 1888 and that after his death "his son gave Calhoun 40 acres" for a new school, a sentence whose pronoun appears garbled. Donor, original acreage, and cause of death are carried as reported and flagged as unverified in this pass.
  7. Hungerford's county era. Orange County Public Schools "took control in 1950" (town reference summary, school history) versus purchased the campus land in 1951 for about $16,000 with an educational-use stipulation (the same summary's land section, drawing on CBS News). Closure: 2009 (Central Florida Public Media, January 2026 and September 2025; Florida History Network) versus 2010 (town reference summary). Carried: takeover 1950-51; closure 2009.
  8. Hurston's birth. Documented: January 7, 1891, Notasulga, Alabama (Hemenway-based biographies). Hurston herself frequently claimed Eatonville as her birthplace and gave later birth years in her own accounts, which is why older reference works list her as born in 1901. The documented version is carried; her claim is preserved in the text because it is evidence of what Eatonville meant to her, not of where she was born.
  9. Lucy Hurston's death. 1904 (standard biographies) versus 1901 (Black Women's Religious Activism web biography, which also calls Hurston nine years old that year, arithmetic that fits neither the 1891 birth nor its own date). 1904 carried; 1901 treated as an error.
  10. Hurston library opening. January 2004 (town reference summary) versus 2006 (Florida History Network). Both carried; unresolved in this pass.
  11. 2020 population. 2,349 (U.S. Census; project outreach plan) versus 2,399 (Florida Backroads Travel). 2,349 carried; 2,399 appears in no census reporting and is treated as an error.
  12. Hungerford parcel size today. About 117 acres in two parcels (GrowthSpotter; Fox 35 Orlando) versus "more than 100 acres" (Central Florida Public Media) versus the 100-acre remnant of the 1951-era 300 acres described in the land record after court reductions (town reference summary). Carried as about 117 acres with the variance noted.
  13. End of the P.E.C. lawsuit. The complaint record shows the court granted the School Board judgment on the pleadings on December 31, 2024; Central Florida Public Media's January 2026 report says the lawsuit "was thrown out in 2025." The court date is carried; the variance is in the secondary reporting.
