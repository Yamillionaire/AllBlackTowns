Lesson Plan (Pilot): The Richest Street in the World

Free beta sample. The pilot lesson plan for the All Black Towns curriculum, built on the Sweet Auburn dossier. It opens with the song, moves into the documents, and closes with discussion and a hands-on task.

Header

  • Lesson title: The Richest Street in the World: How Sweet Auburn Built a Black Economic Capital, and What Threatened It
  • Town(s) covered, State(s): Sweet Auburn historic district, centered on Auburn Avenue, Atlanta, Fulton County, Georgia. Honest fit note, carried from the dossier: Sweet Auburn is a street and district inside Atlanta's Old Fourth Ward, never an incorporated town. This lesson teaches it as what it is, a district that functioned as a civic and economic capital, and that distinction is itself part of the lesson.
  • Unit: Prosperity (primary). The lesson also draws on Attack and Erasure material (the 1906 massacre, highway construction) and Survival and Preservation material (the 1976 landmark designation and the keeping organizations) from the same dossier.
  • Grade band: 9-12 (flagship band). Adaptation notes for 6-8 and for adult self-guided learners appear at the end of this plan.
  • Estimated time: two class periods of about 50 minutes, plus performance task completion time. Self-paced equivalent: about two hours.
  • Materials needed: the Sweet Auburn song (audio, 3:11, from the All Black Towns site or teacher-provided file); printed annotated lyric sheet; printed primary source handout (nomination form excerpts, see Primary Source Activity); claim-checker graphic organizer; timeline event cards (see Performance Task); access to the Sweet Auburn Town Dossier for members who want the full record.

Essential Question

How did one Atlanta street become "the richest Negro street in the world," and why did the forces that concentrated its wealth later scatter it?

Background Brief

Sweet Auburn is the historic Black commercial and cultural district centered on Auburn Avenue, just east of downtown Atlanta, Georgia. It was never an incorporated town. It is a street that became a world.

The avenue began as Wheat Street. In 1893 it was renamed Auburn Avenue, at the request of white petitioners who thought the new name sounded more cosmopolitan. The district's roots are older than the name. Big Bethel AME Church traces its congregation to 1847, organized by enslaved members of a white church, and it is carried in the record as the oldest African American congregation in Atlanta. As Jim Crow law hardened in the 1890s and 1900s, Black Atlantans and their businesses were pushed into the corridor east of downtown, and Auburn Avenue became its anchor. By 1900, ten Black businesses and two Black physicians operated on the street.

Builders followed. In 1904, Henry A. Rucker built the Rucker Building, Atlanta's first Black-owned office building. In 1905, Alonzo Herndon, born enslaved in Walton County, by then the owner of a successful barbershop business, founded an insurance company, the seed of Atlanta Life. In September 1906, white mobs inflamed by inflammatory, unverified newspaper reports attacked Black residents and businesses across Atlanta. At least 25 Black Atlantans were killed and more than 70 people injured. Casualty counts vary by source. Black businesses that had operated in a substantially integrated downtown relocated to Auburn Avenue for safety. Segregation then concentrated a captive Black market on the avenue for half a century.

What rose there was a complete civic and economic capital. Citizens Trust Bank opened on August 16, 1921, founded by five men remembered as the Fervent Five. The Atlanta Daily World, founded on August 5, 1928, became the first successful Black daily newspaper in the United States by 1932. Civic leader John Wesley Dobbs coined the name "Sweet Auburn" and served as the avenue's unofficial "Mayor," organizing voter registration. The Reverend A. D. Williams bought the house at 501 Auburn Avenue in 1909; his grandson, Martin Luther King, Jr., was born there on January 15, 1929. In the peak decades, the avenue's finance rested on what historian Gary Pomerantz calls the "three-legged stool of Black finance": Atlanta Life Insurance, Standard Life Insurance, and Citizens Trust Bank. In 1956, Fortune magazine called Auburn Avenue "the richest Negro street in the world."

The turning came from two directions. The Downtown Connector highway, built through Atlanta's core in the 1950s and early 1960s, divided the district and took its western blocks. Then, from the mid-1960s, as legal segregation fell, shopkeepers and residents dispersed to other parts of the city, and disinvestment followed. The district was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1976, but a 2021 National Park Service assessment found that 47 percent of its historic buildings had been lost since that designation. The churches still worship, the Curb Market still trades, and preservation organizations still work the avenue.

Vocabulary

  1. Jim Crow: the system of laws and customs that enforced racial segregation in the American South. In this lesson, the force that pushed Black businesses and institutions into the Auburn Avenue corridor.
  2. Segregation: the enforced separation of people by race in business, housing, schooling, and public life.
  3. Captive market: a body of customers who effectively have one place to spend their money. Segregation confined Black spending to Black business districts, which concentrated wealth on Auburn Avenue for half a century.
  4. Disinvestment: the long withdrawal of money, maintenance, and services from a place. On Auburn Avenue it followed the dispersal of the 1960s and showed up as population loss, housing decay, and neglect.
  5. National Historic Landmark: a place designated by the Secretary of the Interior for exceptional significance in the nation's history. Sweet Auburn received the designation in 1976.
  6. Primary source: a record created at the time, by people who were there, or an official record of the thing itself. The 1976 landmark nomination form used in this lesson is a primary source for the case its writers made.
  7. Compression: in a song or a story, the folding together of events, or the tightening of a sequence, so the telling fits the form. Compression is honest when it is flagged; it misleads when it silently changes the chronology.

Opening: The Song

Play "Sweet Auburn, Atlanta, Georgia" (3:11) in full, once, with no introduction beyond its title. About the song, stated plainly for students: it is the All Black Towns project's own work. The lyrics were written by Yamil Little; the music and arrangement were generated with the Suno tool. Three recorded versions exist (a studio original and two later remasters) with identical lyrics; this lesson uses the lyrics, which do not change between versions. Like any source, the song gets both respect and checking.

Two guided prompts, on the claim-checker organizer:

  1. What did you hear? List every checkable claim the song makes: every name, date, building, business, and "first" you can catch. Do not worry about order; get them down.
  2. What do you want to check? Star the claims that sound too neat, too big, or out of order. Those stars are the lesson's agenda.

Expected catch list (teacher reference): Dobbs and the "richest" line; 1906 and the Wheat Street renaming in the same breath; Herndon "from slavery" to Atlanta Life; the Rucker Building, 1904; Standard Life; Citizens Trust Bank; the Daily World as "first daily"; Big Bethel, 1847; Dobbs as "mayor"; the King house; the Royal Peacock; decline and vacancies; the national landmark.

Primary Source Activity

The document: National Register of Historic Places Inventory, Nomination Form: Sweet Auburn Historic District (1976). National Archives Catalog, Record Group 079, National Register number 76000631. The dossier's conflict log dates the nomination file to December 8, 1976. Rights: a federal document, public domain, free to print and hand out. Full text: https://catalog.archives.gov/medialz/electronic-records/rg-079/NPS_GA/76000631_NHL.pdf

Handout: the form's description and significance excerpts (teacher selects two to three pages), with the dossier's one-line sourcing note printed on the sheet: what the document is, who produced it, when, and its rights status.

Structured analysis (observe, question, connect):

  • Observe. What kind of document is this, who wrote it, and for what purpose? List three buildings, businesses, or facts the form names. What year is the form itself arguing from, and what is it asking the federal government to do?
  • Question. The form was written to make a case. What does it emphasize, and what does it leave out? Does it discuss the 1906 massacre? The highway that had already cut the district? The decline already underway? What can a document written in 1976 not yet know?
  • Connect. Take one claim from the form and check it against the dossier timeline and against the song. Then read the update the form's writers could not have imagined: a 2021 National Park Service assessment found the district had lost 47 percent of its historic buildings since the 1976 designation, with the Atlanta Life Insurance Company building, the Butler Street YMCA, and the Walden Building standing vacant and at risk. What changed between the form's Auburn Avenue and today's, and what did the designation protect, and fail to protect?

Lyric Analysis

Return to the song with the dossier in hand. Students work down the annotated lyric sheet and mark each line: Verified, Verified with a nuance, Compression, Attribution shifted, or Not yet verified. The verdicts below are the answer guidance; every one of them comes from the dossier's lyric annotation (Dossier Section 7) and conflict log (Dossier Section 9).

  1. Intro: "Dobbs called it honey, richest Black vein in the South." Attribution shifted, and the song corrects itself. Dobbs coined the name "Sweet Auburn." The "richest" wording belongs to Fortune magazine, 1956, as Verse 2 itself says ("Fortune call it the richest strip"). One secondary source (a photo caption) reverses the attribution, and one encyclopedia summary muddles it; the dossier follows the New Georgia Encyclopedia: Dobbs coined the name, Fortune printed "richest." Teaching point: even a songwriter compressing for rhyme leaves the correction in the record, if you read the whole song against the documents.
  2. Verse 1: "1906 riots scatter seeds in the soil / Wheat Street flip to Auburn." Compression. The sequence in the record runs the other way in time: Wheat Street was renamed Auburn Avenue in 1893, Black businesses stood on the street by 1900, and the 1906 massacre then accelerated and concentrated the district. The verse folds thirteen years into two lines. The underlying claim, that 1906 concentrated Black business on the avenue, is verified; the order is the song's, not the record's.
  3. Verse 1: "Herndon from slavery, barber clip to Atlanta Life." Verified core, one open question. Alonzo Herndon was born enslaved, built a successful barbering business, and founded his insurance company in 1905. The dossier carries his description in district accounts as the first Black millionaire. The open question: sources disagree on the company's original name (the New Georgia Encyclopedia and Main Street America say Atlanta Life Insurance Company; a Wikipedia summary says Atlanta Mutual), and the dossier leaves the exact name and any renaming date unresolved. Students should see a fact (founder, year, industry) held firmly while a detail (the corporate name) is honestly unsettled.
  4. Verse 1: "Rucker Building rise, 1904 office crown." Verified. Henry A. Rucker's Rucker Building, 1904, Atlanta's first Black-owned office building.
  5. Verse 1: "Citizens Trust Bank fund the homes and the stores." Verified. Citizens Trust Bank opened on Auburn Avenue on August 16, 1921, founded by the Fervent Five: Heman E. Perry, James A. Robinson, Thomas J. Ferguson, W. H. King, and Henry Dugas (Georgia Historical Society marker).
  6. Verse 1: "Daily World print the wins, first daily Black doors." Verified with a nuance, and the nuance is the lesson. William Alexander Scott II, age 26, founded the paper on Auburn Avenue on August 5, 1928, and by 1932 it had become the first successful Black daily newspaper in the United States, growing from weekly to semi-weekly to triweekly to daily. The record's word is "successful." Students discuss why a careful writer keeps the qualifier and what the unqualified lyric version claims beyond the record.
  7. Verse 1: "Big Bethel AME preach from 1847 roots." Verified as carried, with a flagged outlier. The Georgia Historical Society marker and the encyclopedia tradition date the congregation's organization to 1847; one source gives 1840, which the dossier records as an outlier rather than adopting. Teaching point: an outlier is noted, weighed, and set aside with a reason, not silently averaged in.
  8. Verse 2: "John Wesley Dobbs mayor." Verified as an honorary title. Dobbs was the avenue's unofficial "Mayor of Auburn Avenue," earned through civic leadership and voter registration work; he held no elected office. The dossier also carries a reported figure for his registration drives, more than 10,000 Black voters registered in the 1930s, drawn from a Georgia State University account of an Atlanta History Center podcast, and flags that figure as reported, not yet primary-verified. Accept the figure from students only when they carry the flag with it.
  9. Verse 2: "MLK parsonage cradle the dream he honed," and the intro's "King house." Compression of two true things. The house is the King Birth Home at 501 Auburn Avenue, built in 1895 and purchased in 1909 by the Reverend A. D. Williams, King's grandfather and the pastor of Ebenezer Baptist Church; Martin Luther King, Jr. was born there on January 15, 1929. The song merges the birth home with the parsonage image. Both anchors are real; the single image is the songwriter's.
  10. Verse 2: "SCLC plan marches right off Auburn stone." Verified anchor. The Prince Hall Masonic Temple on Auburn Avenue later housed the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (National Park Service, per the dossier).
  11. Verse 2, the decline passage: "progress pull people, suburbs call 'em away, vacants creep in," and "National landmark stand." Verified arc. From the mid-1960s, as legal barriers fell, avenue shopkeepers moved their businesses elsewhere in the city and residents migrated to Atlanta's west side; the Downtown Connector had already divided the district; disinvestment followed. The National Historic Landmark designation came in 1976, and the 2021 assessment's 47 percent loss figure closes the arc the verse sketches.
  12. Flagged lines, named honestly. "Perry drop Standard Life": Standard Life Insurance belongs to the avenue's documented insurance cluster, one leg of the three-legged stool, but the dossier's annotation flags this line as not separately source-checked in the dossier pass, and students should leave its founding details marked pending. The same annotation flag covers the "Morris Brown College birth," "WERD radio wave," and "Tiger Flowers" lines: all consistent with the district's standard record, not separately verified in the dossier pass. (The dossier's timeline does separately record Morris Brown College's first classes, held at Big Bethel in 1881, which is the documented anchor nearest the song's "birth" line.) A curriculum that teaches sourcing has to show its own seams; these lines are where this one shows them.

Discussion

  1. The record says the 1906 massacre terrorized Black Atlanta and, at the same time, concentrated the district that became Sweet Auburn. How can both be true? What role did the captive market play, and what does that teach about how segregation shaped Black economic life?
  2. John Wesley Dobbs held no office, yet the avenue called him its mayor, and the record credits his registration work with real political weight. Where did his authority come from, and what work did it do that an office could not?
  3. Fortune's praise came in 1956. Within a generation, the avenue was thinning. The dossier gives two causes: a highway built through the district, and dispersal after legal segregation fell. Why would winning the end of segregation weaken the street segregation built? Keep the two causes separate in your answer; which one was a policy choice made about the district, and which was a consequence of freedom of movement?
  4. The landmark designation came in 1976. By 2021, 47 percent of the historic buildings were gone. What does a designation actually protect, and what does it leave unprotected? Who has done the protecting that got done (the dossier names the National Park Service, HDDC, Sweet Auburn Works, the Atlanta Preservation Center, the APEX Museum, and the Auburn Avenue Research Library)?
  5. The song compresses thirteen years into two lines and merges two attributions into one, then corrects one of them three verses later. When is compression honest storytelling, and when does it become misinformation? Who should get to decide, the songwriter, the historian, or the listener with the documents in hand?

Performance Task

The 1976 Brief, with a 2026 Addendum. Three parts, all evidence drawn from the dossier and the lesson's primary source.

Part 1, Timeline rebuild. In teams, students receive a shuffled set of event cards and must order them and caption each with its source type (marker, encyclopedia, federal form, assessment, newspaper history). The card set: 1847, Big Bethel congregation organized; 1893, Wheat Street renamed Auburn Avenue; 1900, ten Black businesses and two Black physicians on the street; 1904, Rucker Building; 1905, Herndon founds his insurance company; 1906, the Atlanta Race Massacre (September); 1921, Citizens Trust Bank opens (August 16); 1928, the Atlanta World founded (August 5); 1929, Martin Luther King, Jr. born at 501 Auburn Avenue (January 15); 1932, the Daily World becomes the first successful Black daily in the United States; 1956, Fortune's "richest Negro street in the world"; 1950s to early 1960s, the Downtown Connector divides the district; mid-1960s onward, dispersal and disinvestment; 1976, National Historic Landmark designation; 2021, the NPS assessment finds 47 percent of historic buildings lost since 1976.

Part 2, The brief. Each student (or team) writes a one-page landmark brief in the dossier's own format: a key facts box (district, county, status, known for), a significance argument built on at least three dated pieces of evidence from the timeline, and one line labeled "Not established in the record," naming something the dossier could not verify (examples: a district-level population figure, peak or today, which the dossier marks NOT ESTABLISHED; the original corporate name of Herndon's 1905 company). The brief may argue for the whole district or for a single institution on the avenue (a church, the bank, the newspaper, the Rucker Building).

Part 3, The addendum. One paragraph, dated today: given the 47 percent loss figure, what should preservation do next on Auburn Avenue, and which of the keeping organizations named in the dossier is best placed to do it? Argue from the record, not from sentiment.

Assessment

Rubric notes for the teacher, four criteria:

  • Evidence use. Claims are tied to dated items from the dossier or the nomination form. The strongest work quotes the record's own qualifiers ("first successful," "at least 25") instead of upgrading them.
  • Sourcing. The student distinguishes the primary source (the 1976 form) from secondary accounts, notices what the form omits, and carries rights and authorship correctly (a federal public-domain document; a song with human-written lyrics and generated music).
  • Reasoning. The two turning points are kept separate and causation is handled carefully: 1906 concentrated, the highway divided, dispersal and disinvestment followed. Answers that merge the causes into "things declined" have not done the analysis.
  • Handling of uncertainty. Credit, do not penalize, the honest flag: an outlier date noted and set aside, a reported figure carried with its flag, a "not established" line included because the record is silent. Smoothing a conflict over is the one move this curriculum treats as an error.

Self-check list for individual learners:

  • I can name three institutions on the avenue and give each a date.
  • I can explain, in order, why 1893, 1906, and 1956 matter, and I know which of them the song compresses.
  • I can correct the song's intro using the song's own second verse.
  • I can say what the word "successful" is doing in "first successful Black daily."
  • I can name one thing the record has not established about Sweet Auburn, and say why the dossier refuses to guess at it.
  • I can say what the 1976 designation protected and what it did not.

Trauma-Informed Teaching Note

Required for this lesson: it contains the September 1906 Atlanta Race Massacre, in which white mobs killed at least 25 Black Atlantans and injured more than 70 people, on the dossier's conservative sourced figures.

  • Preview, do not surprise. Tell students before the song and the brief that the lesson includes a massacre, what it was, and the figures the lesson will use. The same preview heads the self-guided version, in print, before the Background Brief.
  • Choose the words with the class. The project's own site usage, and much recent writing, says "Atlanta Race Massacre"; period sources and many summaries say "race riot." The dossier uses both, sourced. Naming the choice, and why it matters, is part of the lesson, not a distraction from it.
  • Keep the detail at the level of the record. The lesson needs the cause (inflammatory, unverified newspaper reports), the toll as sourced, and the consequence (relocation and concentration). It does not need graphic description, and the lesson's primary source is deliberately a federal nomination form, not atrocity imagery. The one photograph in the dossier's source pack, a circa 1945 Auburn Avenue street scene held by the Atlanta History Center, is copyrighted, link only, and optional.
  • No reenactment, no role-play of the violence, no debate format on whether it happened or what it meant. Discussion stays analytical, and Question 1 routes the emotion where it belongs: into what the community built in answer.
  • Opt-out with dignity. A student who needs to step back from the massacre segment can work the finance-and-institutions track instead (the three-legged stool and the timeline cards) and rejoin for the landmark and preservation segments. Same performance task, no public explanation required.
  • End on the keeping, not the killing. Close with the district today, from the dossier: the churches still worship, the Curb Market still trades, the King birth home and Ebenezer stand inside a National Historical Park, the Sweet Auburn Festival runs each year, and named organizations are doing named preservation work. The last word of the lesson is survival with an address.

Grade-Band Adaptation Notes

Grades 6-8

  • Background Brief: read aloud in three chunks (the street forms; the builders; the turning), pausing on the timeline strip. Keep the same facts; shorten by dropping the Fervent Five roster and the Daily World's growth steps, not by simplifying the causation.
  • Vocabulary: five terms (Jim Crow, segregation, captive market, National Historic Landmark, primary source).
  • Primary source: swap the nomination form for the Georgia Historical Society's Citizens Trust Bank marker text (a public marker, text published online by GHS, per the Dossier Section 8 source pack): short, concrete, and it names the bank, the date, and the five founders. Keep one nomination form excerpt as a teacher-led read for classes ready for it.
  • Lyric Analysis: four lines only (the intro attribution, the 1906 sequence, Citizens Trust, and the Daily World's "successful"), with the same verdict categories.
  • Discussion: Questions 1, 2, and 5.
  • Performance Task: Part 1 timeline rebuild, plus a poster or infographic of the three-legged stool with one evidence sentence per leg. Part 2 shrinks to a five-line landmark brief with the "not established" line kept; Part 3 becomes a single sentence of advice to a preservation organization.
  • Trauma-informed note applies in full, with the preview given verbally and in writing to families where district practice requires it.

Adult self-guided

  • Work the lesson in order, at your own pace; the trauma-informed preview stands at the head of the Background Brief in the self-guided edition.
  • Replace Discussion with written responses to the same five questions; the Self-Check List in Assessment is your assessment.
  • Performance Task: do all three parts in writing; the brief format is the dossier's, so a strong brief doubles as a model of the membership's core product.
  • Extensions, all from the dossier's own source pack: read the National Park Service's "The Sweet Auburn Community" page (public domain text; its images are licensed, link only); compare the short Sweet Auburn blurb on the All Black Towns site against the dossier and find the compression the dossier's conflict log flags (the blurb's "rose after the 1906 Atlanta Race Massacre" against businesses on the street by 1900); and note the dossier's open questions (district population not established; Herndon Building construction year not verified; the original name of Herndon's company) as the project's honesty standard in action.
