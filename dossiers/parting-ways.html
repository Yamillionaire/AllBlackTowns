Key Facts Box

  • Founded: 1792. Basis: on March 12, 1792, the Town of Plymouth voted and granted a strip of town land on the easterly side of the sheep pasture to such persons as would clear it within three years; the four veterans took up the grant, and the settlement’s official life is dated from that vote (Pilgrim Hall Museum, "Long Road to Freedom"; James Deetz, "Parting Ways"). The founding is layered: Plato Turner had bought a house on the site in 1779, and the other three founders had built houses there by 1790, so Black residence on the ground predates the 1792 vote by more than a decade.
  • Founded by: four African American veterans of the Revolutionary War and their families: Cato Howe, Plato Turner, Prince Goodwin, and Quamany Quash.
  • Incorporated: never. Parting Ways was a settlement on Town of Plymouth land inside the Town of Plymouth; it had no municipal existence of its own at any point in the record consulted.
  • Land: a strip about twenty rods wide and about a mile and a half long on the easterly side of the town sheep pasture, conventionally given as 94 acres (Deetz, "Parting Ways," quoting the March 12, 1792 vote). The grant was provisional: there is no record of an outright grant of title, and the men resided with the town’s permission until 1824; Cato Howe’s own 1820 pension schedule lists his real estate as "None" (Deetz).
  • Peak population: no peak figure is established in the record, and none is carried here. The documented points are these: the settlement began with at least four Black families (Deetz, "Parting Ways"); and of the 54 African Americans living in Plymouth in 1790, only 13 lived autonomously from white households, and all 13 of those individuals lived at Parting Ways (Karen Hutchins-Keim, quoted in Historical Digression).
  • Population today: no residents. The site is a cemetery, an interpretive display, and wooded town open space in West Plymouth.
  • Known for: one of the earliest independent free Black communities in New England, founded by Black veterans of the Revolution on land they cleared under a town grant; and, in scholarship, the site of James Deetz’s landmark 1975 and 1976 excavations and Karen Hutchins-Keim’s 2015 re-examination, a foundational case in African American historical archaeology (Deetz, In Small Things Forgotten; Hutchins-Keim, "Parting Ways Revisited," 2015). Part of the land was added to the National Register of Historic Places on April 19, 1979, as the Parting Ways Archeological District, reference number 79000367.
  • The song: "Parting Ways, in Plymouth, Massachusetts." Its full lyrics are not printed in this dossier yet: the lyric text is not in the project’s archive, and the gap is recorded rather than filled with invented text.

Introduction

  • Town name, State: Parting Ways, Massachusetts
  • County: Plymouth County
  • Status: abandoned settlement; the site survives as a town-owned cemetery and archaeological district in West Plymouth. Part of the land was added to the National Register of Historic Places on April 19, 1979, as the Parting Ways Archeological District, reference number 79000367.
  • Dossier version: draft v0.1, 2026-11-09

Parting Ways was not a town in the municipal sense and never claimed to be one. It was a small free Black settlement of four veteran households and their descendants on town land at the edge of Plymouth, Massachusetts, known in its own time as New Guinea. There was no incorporation, no town government, no post office, and no separate census place, and none is invented here. The settlement’s record is documented on its own terms, as the project does for its other settlement entries.

What Parting Ways left is unusually rich in a different way: a town vote, pension files, probate inventories, maps, one long newspaper visit in 1895, standing graves, and one of the most cited archaeological excavations in African American historical archaeology, re-examined a generation later. The depth in this dossier comes from those sources, read closely. Where the record runs out, this dossier says so rather than padding.

Timeline: the story at a glance

1775Cato Howe enlists with the 2nd Massachusetts Regiment; he is present at the Battle of Bunker Hill on June 17, 1775 (Deetz, "Parting Ways"; Pilgrim Hall Museum).
1776Plato Turner enlists with the 3rd Massachusetts Regiment and serves in upstate New York during the Canadian campaign of 1776 and 1777, at the Battle of Valcour Bay on Lake Champlain and at Saratoga. Quamany Quash also enlists in 1776, in the service of the continent for three years (Historical Digression; Old Colony Memorial, 2021).
1779Plato Turner, between enlistments and newly declared a freeman, returns to Plymouth briefly and purchases Seth Fuller’s house at the Parting Ways crossroads. The dwelling becomes the nucleus of the settlement (Historical Digression, citing Hutchins-Keim).
By 1790Cato Howe, Quamany Quash, and Prince Goodwin have built houses at Parting Ways alongside Turner’s purchased house. Of the 54 African Americans living in Plymouth in 1790, only 13 lived autonomously from white households, and all 13 of those individuals lived at Parting Ways (Hutchins-Keim, via Historical Digression).
March 12, 1792the Town of Plymouth votes and grants a strip of land about twenty rods wide and about a mile and a half long on the easterly side of the sheep pasture, to such persons as will clear the same in the term of three years (town vote, quoted in Deetz, "Parting Ways," and in Pilgrim Hall Museum, "Long Road to Freedom"). This is the founding act this dossier and the project carry.
1818Cato Howe applies to the federal government for a pension based on reduced circumstances, and the pension is granted; Plato Turner also applies for a pension in 1818 (Deetz, "Parting Ways").
1820Howe files a schedule of his whole personal property in support of his pension: real estate none, and his listed goods total 27 dollars (Deetz, "Parting Ways," from Howe’s pension file). In the same year Quamany Quash receives his pension, eight dollars monthly (Deetz).
1824Cato Howe dies, and his estate is probated. In the same year the town authorizes the sale of the Parting Ways property; the land is not in fact sold and remains the property of the town (Deetz, "Parting Ways").
1833Quamany Quash dies (Deetz, "Parting Ways"; Old Colony Memorial, 2021).
December 8, 1895the Boston Globe publishes J. N. Taylor’s "Jim Burr’s Life, History of Plymouth’s Colored Citizen," the fullest eyewitness account of the settlement in its last inhabited years: one house standing, occupied by James Burr, and five cellar holes yawning in the ground where the other dwellings had stood (Historical Digression, citing Taylor; Deetz, "Parting Ways").
About 1908the Turner-Burr house, the last remnant of the settlement, burns, and the settlement’s inhabited life ends (Deetz, "Parting Ways").
1975 and 1976archaeological excavations at Parting Ways under Dr. James Deetz of Brown University and Plimoth Plantation, sponsored by the Plymouth Bicentennial Committee on Afro-American History, with a crew including local African American young people (Deetz, "Parting Ways").
April 19, 1979part of the Parting Ways land is added to the National Register of Historic Places as the Parting Ways Archeological District, reference number 79000367 (National Register Information System).
2012the Parting Ways Museum, the Town of Plymouth, and the Plymouth County Convention and Visitors Bureau collaborate to install a large interpretive sign at the site, and a granite marker stands at the cemetery over the graves of the first four founders (Historical Digression).
2015Karen A. Hutchins-Keim publishes "Parting Ways Revisited: Archaeology at a Nineteenth-Century African-American Community in Plymouth, Massachusetts," re-examining Deetz’s evidence a generation later (Hutchins-Keim, 2015).

The Song

Parting Ways is a rural African American settlement in Plymouth, Massachusetts, founded in 1792 when the town granted about 94 acres to Black veterans of the American Revolution. It remains one of the earliest independent free Black communities in New England, carried by four founding families and their descendants for more than a century.

1. Founding

Parting Ways was founded twice, and the dossier keeps the two foundings separate because the sources do: once in fact, by men moving onto the land between 1779 and 1790, and once in law, by a town vote on March 12, 1792. The project carries 1792 as the founding year, as documented in the project’s release plan, and that is the correct formal date, because 1792 is when the settlement acquired its official life (Pilgrim Hall Museum, "Long Road to Freedom," which states that the New Guinea Settlement at Parting Ways "began its official life in 1792"). But a curriculum that taught only the vote would miss what the men themselves did, which is that they were already there.

The ground first. The settlement stood at a fork where the roads from Plymouth toward Plympton and toward Carver parted, in scrub oak and pine on the far outskirts of town, near the Kingston line (Historical Digression; Deetz, "Parting Ways"). Its soil was sandy and poor for farming; unable to sell the land off, the town had retained it as a common sheep pasture (Historical Digression). Deetz, writing after walking and digging the tract, calls the soil gravelly and singularly unfertile, and Pilgrim Hall’s executive director puts the pasture point bluntly a generation later: the land "was really only good for grazing sheep" (Deetz, "Parting Ways"; Donna Curtin, quoted in Old Colony Memorial, 2021). During the middle to late eighteenth century several poor and transient families squatted on the pasture, including the family of Seth Fuller, who in the 1760s came to some tacit agreement with the town and was allowed to build a small house at the crossroads (Historical Digression, citing Hutchins-Keim, "Parting Ways Revisited," page 123). That Fuller layer matters: one of the structures Deetz later excavated and interpreted was in fact built by the Anglo-American settler Seth Fuller, a complication Hutchins-Keim insists on and the next chapter carries (Hutchins-Keim, via Historical Digression).

The founders were four Plymouth men of African descent who had served in the Revolutionary War: Cato Howe, Plato Turner, Prince Goodwin, and Quamany Quash, with their families (Deetz; Pilgrim Hall Museum; Wikipedia). Their full sourced biographies are in the People chapter of this dossier. What belongs here is the sequence by which they assembled the settlement, because the sequence is the founding.

Plato Turner moved first. Held in slavery before the war, Turner enlisted with the 3rd Massachusetts Regiment in 1776, served through the Canadian campaign, Valcour Bay, and Saratoga, finished his first enlistment in 1779, and was declared a freeman; in that interval he returned to Plymouth and purchased Seth Fuller’s house at Parting Ways (Historical Digression, citing Hutchins-Keim). He then re-enlisted, served at West Point, and was discharged in 1783, at which point the Fuller dwelling became the nucleus of Plymouth’s New Guinea (Historical Digression). The purchase is the settlement’s first documented Black land transaction on the site, a private purchase from a white occupant, thirteen years before the town vote: the settlement begins with a veteran buying a house with, so far as the record shows, his own means, on land the town still owned (Historical Digression; Hutchins-Keim).

By 1790, Howe, Quash, and Goodwin had built houses at Parting Ways as well (Historical Digression). The settlement that the 1792 vote then recognized was therefore not a paper colony: it was four households already standing on a crossroads, built by the men who would clear the strip. Hutchins-Keim’s demographic point gives the founding its full weight: of the 54 African Americans living in Plymouth in 1790, only 13 lived autonomously from white households, and all 13 of those individuals lived at Parting Ways; in her words, the households at Parting Ways "marked an important milestone in the development of free black communities in the area" (Hutchins-Keim, quoted in Historical Digression). In a town where most Black people still lived inside white households, as servants or dependents, Parting Ways was, entire, the autonomous Black Plymouth of 1790.

The vote itself, March 12, 1792, is preserved in its operative words, quoted by both Deetz and Pilgrim Hall: the town "voted and granted a strip of land about twenty rods wide and about a mile and a half long on the easterly side of the sheep pasture, to such persons as will clear the same in the term of three years" (Deetz, "Parting Ways"; Pilgrim Hall Museum). Twenty rods is a tenth of a mile; a strip that wide and a mile and a half long is the long, thin tract the sources conventionally total as 94 acres (Deetz). The vote names no grantee. Pilgrim Hall’s exhibition text names all four as the persons meant: "The ‘persons’ were four African-American veterans of the Revolutionary War: Cato Howe, Plato Turner, Quamany Quash and Prince Goodwin" (Pilgrim Hall Museum). What no source disputes is that the four and their families are who cleared it, built on it, and lived on it.

The terms deserve the curriculum’s full attention, because they define the settlement’s whole later history. The grant was conditional (clear the land within three years) and, in the event, provisional in a deeper sense: Deetz states flatly that "the ninety-four acres of land on which these four men lived were provisionally granted to Cato Howe in 1792, although there is no record of an outright grant of title to him," and that the four "cleared the property, built houses, and resided there with the town’s permission until 1824" (Deetz, "Parting Ways"). Howe’s own 1820 pension schedule, listing his real estate as "None" twenty-eight years after the vote, is the founders’ own paperwork confirming that they never owned the soil under their houses (Deetz).

Why this place, and why then, is documented well enough to teach without speculation. The men came home from the Continental Army to a Massachusetts that had ended slavery by court decision in 1783 but, in Deetz’s sentence, "did little or nothing to provide for their new needs, and subsistence, employment, and housing were difficult to come by" (Deetz, "Parting Ways"). Deetz’s framing chapter makes the structural point: while the state saw to it that these people were free, it provided almost nothing for the freed. A town sheep pasture that no buyer wanted, offered on clearing terms to men with no capital, was both an opportunity and a sentence: autonomy, at the price of farming gravel. The settlement’s name for itself in the records of others was New Guinea, which Deetz notes was a fairly common term across Anglo-America for separate Black settlements, and which the ethicarch.org history calls a common racial tag applied to African American freeperson communities (Deetz; ethicarch.org). The name the place kept, Parting Ways, is geographic, from the fork in the road (Deetz; Pilgrim Hall Museum). Both names are carried in this dossier; "New Guinea" appears in the sources as a period label imposed on Black settlements generally, not as a name the founders are recorded choosing.

The founding families, so far as the record names them, are the founders’ households: Howe’s wives Althea and later Lucy; Quash’s wife Ellen and their two sons; Turner’s widow Rachel and their son James; Goodwin’s wife and five children (Deetz, "Parting Ways"; the People chapter). The first institutions planted, in the town template’s sense, were none that the record names: no church, school, lodge, or store at Parting Ways appears in any source consulted, and this dossier records that absence rather than assuming frontier institutions into existence. What the founders planted was households, clustered close together at the center of the tract, a pattern Deetz read as a corporate, communal settlement choice against the scattered Anglo-American farmstead pattern of the same years (Deetz). For Parting Ways, the household cluster was the institution.
