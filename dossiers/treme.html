Key Facts Box

  • Founded: 1810. Claude Tremé's plantation land was sold to the City of New Orleans in 1810 and subdivided as Faubourg Tremé, the city's first subdivision, with lots bought by free people of color (Louisiana historical marker for Faubourg Tremé; National Register nomination material for the Jean Louis Rabassa House).
  • Founded by: no single founder in the town sense. The landholder was Claude Tremé, a French hat maker and real estate developer who sold lots from 1798 and the remainder to the city in 1810. The neighborhood as a Black community was founded in effect by its buyers and builders: the free people of color who bought lots, built houses, and planted its institutions (Louisiana historical marker for Faubourg Tremé).
  • Incorporated: never. Tremé was a faubourg, a suburb laid out outside the original city and later absorbed into New Orleans. It has never had a municipal government of its own.
  • Peak population: not established as a historic peak. The highest figure in the modern census series consulted is 8,853 in the 2000 U.S. Census (U.S. Census Bureau figures via The Data Center).
  • Population today: 4,590 in the 2020 U.S. Census, with the neighborhood recorded as 56.3 percent Black or African American in 2020 (U.S. Census Bureau figures via The Data Center).
  • Known for: Congo Square and the Sunday gatherings of enslaved Africans that kept African music, dance, and market culture alive in public; St. Augustine Church (founded 1841), described by its parish as the oldest Black Catholic parish in the United States; a community of free people of color who owned property and worked as craftsmen, artists, musicians, entrepreneurs, doctors, and teachers; benevolent societies, brass bands, and second line culture; and a central place in the birth of jazz (Louisiana historical marker for Faubourg Tremé; 64 Parishes, Historic New Orleans Collection; St. Augustine Church history).
  • The song: "Tremé, Louisiana," this town's song in the All Black Towns series.

Introduction

  • Town name, State: Tremé, Louisiana
  • Parish: Orleans Parish
  • Status: historic neighborhood of New Orleans, just north of the French Quarter across North Rampart Street. Tremé is a neighborhood, not a separate town: it was never incorporated and has never had a municipal government of its own.
  • Dossier version: draft v0.1, 2026-11-09

Tremé is a historic neighborhood of New Orleans, just north of the French Quarter across North Rampart Street, and it is widely considered the oldest African American neighborhood in the United States. It began as Faubourg Tremé, the city's first subdivision: Claude Tremé sold lots on his plantation property from 1798, and in 1810 the City of New Orleans bought the remaining land and laid it out in lots.

The neighborhood was founded in effect by its buyers and builders: the free people of color who bought lots, built houses, and planted its institutions. They worked as craftsmen, artists, musicians, entrepreneurs, doctors, and teachers, and they held their culture in public: the Sunday gatherings of enslaved Africans at Congo Square kept African music, dance, and market culture alive, and St. Augustine Church, founded in 1841, is described by its parish as the oldest Black Catholic parish in the United States.

From Tremé came benevolent societies, brass bands, and second lines, and a central place in the birth of jazz. From Tremé also came Homer Plessy, whose 1892 arrest in a planned test case carried the fight against segregation to the Supreme Court. The chapters that follow take the story in order, from the founding to the neighborhood today.

Timeline: the story at a glance

1798 to 1810Claude Tremé sells lots on his plantation property, 37 lots in total, to buyers including free people of color (Tanesse map scholarship).
1810the remaining Tremé land is sold to the City of New Orleans for $40,000 and subdivided as Faubourg Tremé, the city's first subdivision (Tanesse map scholarship; Louisiana historical marker for Faubourg Tremé).
1812city surveyor Jacques Tanesse lays out the original plan of Faubourg Tremé (National Register nomination material for the Jean Louis Rabassa House).
1817a New Orleans ordinance restricts all assemblies of enslaved people for dancing and merriment to one appointed place: the ground known as Congo Square (64 Parishes, "Congo Square," Historic New Orleans Collection).
1841free people of color receive permission to build St. Augustine Church; the church is dedicated on October 9, 1842 (St. Augustine Church history).
June 7, 1892Homer Plessy boards a whites-only car of the East Louisiana Railroad at Press Street and is arrested, in the Comité des Citoyens' planned test case (Preservation Resource Center of New Orleans).
May 18, 1896the U.S. Supreme Court decides Plessy v. Ferguson against Plessy, 7 to 1, upholding separate but equal.
Around 1900the first strains of jazz are heard at dances at Economy Hall, with Buddy Bolden's cornet leading (A Closer Walk, New Orleans Music Map, "Economy Hall").
Late 1960sthe elevated Interstate 10 Claiborne Expressway is built over North Claiborne Avenue, above the neighborhood's oak-lined neutral ground (Smithsonian Magazine, 2021).
1970the first New Orleans Jazz and Heritage Festival is held at Congo Square (64 Parishes, "Congo Square").
1993Congo Square is listed in the National Register of Historic Places (64 Parishes, "Congo Square").
August 29, 2005Hurricane Katrina strikes and the federal levees fail, flooding some 80 percent of New Orleans; Tremé suffers minor to moderate flooding (The Data Center, 2021; Wikipedia, "Tremé").
2020the U.S. Census counts 4,590 people in the neighborhood, 56.3 percent Black or African American (U.S. Census Bureau via The Data Center).

The Song

Tremé is a New Orleans neighborhood just north of the French Quarter, widely considered the nation's oldest African American neighborhood: founded in 1810 as Faubourg Tremé, a center for free people of color who could own property, anchored by Congo Square and St. Augustine Church, and a cradle of early jazz.
