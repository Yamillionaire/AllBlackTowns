Key Facts Box

  • Founded: c. 1825, carried as DOCUMENTED. In 1825 John and Elizabeth Whitehead subdivided their farmland and sold it as lots; on September 27, 1825, Andrew Williams bought three lots and Epiphany Davis bought twelve, and the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church bought six more the same week. The "c." is carried honestly: the sources give both the single dated first-purchase day and a community that formed across the sales that followed, through 1832.
  • Founded by: no single founder in the town sense. The first documented buyers were Andrew Williams, a 25-year-old African American shoeshiner (also described as a bootblack, later a cartman), and Epiphany Davis, a store clerk and trustee of the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church, both members of the New York African Society for Mutual Relief; the AME Zion Church itself was the third founding buyer. The landholders who subdivided and sold the lots were John and Elizabeth Whitehead.
  • Incorporated: never. Seneca Village was a private land subdivision and community in Manhattan, not a municipality; it had no incorporation, no charter, no mayor, and no government of its own.
  • Peak population: NOT ESTABLISHED as a single settled number. The sources give a range for the village at or near its largest, all tied to the 1855 record but counted on different bases: approximately 225 people in 1855, on an analysis of census, map, and tax records (Central Park Conservancy); 264 people in the 1855 New York State Census as reported in secondary accounts; over 260 in 1855 in the excavation team's own account; and around 300 in a later retrospective summary. On the Conservancy's basis the composition was roughly two-thirds African American, one-third Irish immigrant, and a small number of German descent. No figure is moved between bases.
  • Population today: zero as a village. The site is Central Park landscape along the park's western perimeter from West 82nd to West 89th Street: rolling hills, winding paths, trees, playgrounds, and rock outcrops, with no discernible above-ground trace that people lived there.
  • Known for: Manhattan's first significant community of Black property owners, and the largest concentration of African American landowners in pre-Civil War New York City in the Central Park Conservancy's research framing; a community where land ownership let some Black men meet New York State's $250 freehold voting requirement at a time when that requirement applied only to Black voters; three churches, a public school, and burial grounds built by the residents themselves; and a destruction by eminent domain for Central Park in 1857 that was then largely forgotten for more than a century.
  • The song: "Seneca Village, New York," this town's song in the All Black Towns series.

Introduction

  • Town name, State: Seneca Village, New York
  • County: New York County, in the borough of Manhattan, New York City. The settlement stood near the present Upper West Side, approximately bounded by Central Park West and the projected lines of 82nd Street, 89th Street, and Seventh Avenue; in present park terms, the site runs along the park's western perimeter from West 82nd to West 89th Street.
  • Status: destroyed. The village existed from 1825 to 1857, when the City of New York acquired all private property within it through eminent domain, the residents were forced to leave, and their houses were torn down for the construction of Central Park.
  • Dossier version: draft v0.1, 2026-11-09

Seneca Village was not an incorporated town and never had a municipal government of its own. It was a free Black landowning community in Manhattan, founded in 1825 on subdivided farmland in what is now Central Park, and destroyed in 1857 when the city took the land for the park by eminent domain. In the Central Park Conservancy's research framing, it was the largest concentration of African American landowners in pre-Civil War New York City, and Manhattan's first significant community of Black property owners.

The founding is dated to a single day in the deed-based accounts. In 1825 John and Elizabeth Whitehead divided their farmland into lots for sale, and on September 27, 1825, Andrew Williams, a 25-year-old African American bootblack, bought three lots for $125, and Epiphany Davis, a trustee of the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church, bought twelve lots for $578; the AME Zion Church bought six more lots the same week. Both men belonged to the New York African Society for Mutual Relief. The purchase was also a legal act: since 1821, New York had required Black men alone to hold a $250 freehold estate in order to vote, so land in Seneca Village, held long enough and clear of debts, was the ballot. And the founding came first: when Williams bought in September 1825, slavery was still legal in New York, two years before emancipation was completed in the state on July 4, 1827.

The village the buyers built held some 50 homes, three churches, a public school, and burial grounds by the mid-1850s, on ground its residents worked with gardens, barns, and livestock. In July 1853 the Central Park Act set aside the park tract and authorized the taking; for two years the residents protested and sued, and by October 1, 1857 the last holdouts had been removed and the buildings were razed. The village was then largely forgotten for more than a century. The chapters that follow take the story in order, from the founding to the town today.

Timeline: the story at a glance

1799New York State passes the Act for the Gradual Abolition of Slavery. It frees no living enslaved person; children born to enslaved mothers after July 4, 1799 are to be free after a long indentured service.
March 31, 1817New York passes the act providing that enslaved people born before July 4, 1799 will be free on July 4, 1827, setting the emancipation date that falls two years after Seneca Village's founding purchases.
1821New York's revised constitution removes the property requirement for white men while imposing on Black men alone a requirement to hold a freehold estate worth $250, clear of debts, in order to vote. This is the legal fact that made Seneca Village land ownership a path to the ballot.
1824John Whitehead purchases the farmland that he and Elizabeth Whitehead will subdivide the following year.
September 27, 1825Andrew Williams buys three lots from the Whiteheads for $125, and Epiphany Davis buys twelve lots for $578, on the same day; the AME Zion Church buys six additional lots the same week.
July 4, 1827slavery ends in New York State under the 1817 act, two years after the village's founding purchases.
1825 to 1832the Whiteheads sell about half of their land parcels to African American buyers; by 1832 at least 24 lots have been sold to African Americans, and by the early 1830s there are approximately 10 homes in the village.
1830sresidents of York Hill, a nearby settlement, are forced to move for construction of a basin for the Croton Distributing Reservoir, and many migrate to Seneca Village; the reservoir's granite walls become the village's eastern landmark.
1837the African Union Church purchases lots in Seneca Village, about 100 feet from the AME Zion Church property; the congregation numbers 50.
Mid-1840sa branch of the African Free School opens next to the African Union Church; by the 1850s it has become Colored School No. 3 in the public school system, serving 75 students under teacher Caroline W. Simpson.
1845of the Black New Yorkers eligible to vote under the $250 freehold rule, 10 live in Seneca Village, out of about 100 eligible citywide.
1849a wooden church for All Angels', the village's racially mixed congregation, is built at 84th Street.
1853the AME Zion congregation builds a church building in Seneca Village, with a school in its basement.
July 1853the New York State Legislature passes the Central Park Act, setting aside the tract from 59th to 106th Streets between Fifth and Eighth Avenues for the park and authorizing the taking of the land.
1855the New York State Census and Egbert Viele's topographical map record the village at or near its maximum size: about 50 homes, three churches, burial grounds, and the school.
1855 to 1857for two years, residents protest and file lawsuits to halt the taking of their land.
October 1, 1857city officials report that the last holdouts living on land that was to become Central Park have been removed; by the end of 1857, all residents have had to leave, and the village's buildings are razed.
1992Roy Rosenzweig and Elizabeth Blackmar publish The Park and the People: A History of Central Park, returning Seneca Village to public attention after more than a century of being largely forgotten.
Summer 2011the Institute for the Exploration of Seneca Village History excavates the site over eight weeks, uncovering house foundations and cellar deposits and collecting several thousand artifacts.
June 2024Envisioning Seneca Village launches on Juneteenth, presenting an interactive 3D model of the village as it might have looked in the spring of 1855.

The Song

Seneca Village was a free Black settlement in what is now Central Park, from about 1825 to 1857. New York seized and demolished it by eminent domain for the park. Land ownership there let some Black men meet the state's voting test.
