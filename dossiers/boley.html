  • Founded by: Thomas M. 1. Founding

Boley began as a railroad townsite with an argument behind it. In the spring of 1903, in a discussion about whether Black people were capable of self-government, J. B. Boley, a roadmaster with the Fort Smith and Western Railway, argued that they had simply never had a fair chance; the town that resulted was, in Booker T. Washington's telling, laid out to put that claim to the test (Washington, "Boley, a Negro Town in the West," Outlook, January 4, 1908; the story is also carried in the National Register nomination).

The site was chosen on the Fort Smith and Western line, roughly halfway between Paden and Castle in what was then the Creek Nation, Indian Territory (Oklahoma Historical Society). The land was the 160-acre allotment of Abigail Barnett, who had inherited it from her father James Barnett, a Creek freedman (National Register nomination). Lake Moore, a white attorney and speculator, contributed the initial $500 needed to lease Barnett's land for five years (town history, summarized in Wikipedia from the scholarly literature; BlackPast names the same three men as the founding group). Thomas M. Haynes, a Black farmer and entrepreneur from Texas, was chosen as townsite manager (National Register nomination; Oklahoma Historical Society marker).

The chronology of the founding season matters because the sources date three different events. The townsite was established in August 1903 (Oklahoma Historical Society marker). After a post office was approved in July 1903, the town officially opened for settlement on September 26, 1903, beginning as a camp of Black railroad construction hands (National Register nomination; town history). Haynes advertised the town across the South and Southwest as a place where Black people could govern themselves, and settlers came from Georgia, Texas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, and Florida (National Register nomination). On September 22, 1904, a formal opening of the town was held (National Register nomination). The founding families named in the nomination, the Barnetts, Walkers, Graysons, and Johnsons, became the town's leading citizens (National Register nomination). Additions brought the townsite to about 300 acres (National Register nomination).

One of the most influential early settlers was David J. Turner, a businessman who served repeatedly as mayor and councilman and worked as a pharmacist and, later, bank president (town history). Hilliard Taylor built a thriving cotton ginning business (town history). Boley was incorporated on May 11, 1905 (Oklahoma Historical Society marker).

2. Building the Town

The town's own newspaper was its recruiting engine. The Boley Progress, a weekly, began in 1905, and the paper and promotional campaigns circulated through the South and drew formerly enslaved people and their children to the new town (Oklahoma Historical Society). At Oklahoma statehood in 1907, Boley sheltered 824 individuals within the town (Oklahoma Historical Society).

Banking came early and stayed central:

  • 1906: at a meeting of the Business Men's League of Boley, the Farmers and Merchants Bank of Boley was organized, with planned capital stock of $15,000; its founders included T. M. Haynes and D. J. Turner (Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City, "A Great Moral and Social Force," a history of Black banking).
  • Late 1907: a second bank, Boley Bank and Trust, was founded by E. L. Lugrande (Kansas City Fed).
  • Early 1910: Boley Bank and Trust merged into the Farmers and Merchants Bank (Kansas City Fed).
  • 1921: the First National Bank of Boley opened and became the first Black-owned bank in the United States to hold a national charter; its founders included J. D. Nelson and Sebrone Jones King (Kansas City Fed). The charter distinction is often attached to the Farmers and Merchants Bank in popular retellings; see conflict 6.

Boley also owned its utilities: its own electrical generating plant, water system, and ice plant (Oklahoma Historical Society), and it is widely described as home to the first Black-owned electric company (African American Registry; town histories). It supported two colleges, Creek-Seminole College and the Methodist Episcopal College (Oklahoma Historical Society). Booker T. Washington's 1908 account, written from his 1905 visit, recorded two banks, two cotton gins, a newspaper, a hotel, and the Creek-Seminole College and Agricultural Institute, and put the population at about 2,500 (Outlook, January 4, 1908; the population basis differs from the census counts, see conflict 4).

Civic and fraternal life had a monumental address. The Masonic Grand Lodge completed a Masonic Temple around 1912; at the time it was said to be the tallest building between Okmulgee and Oklahoma City (Oklahoma Historical Society). Churches of every denomination, schools public and private, fraternal clubs, a railroad depot, a post office, and a telephone company are all recorded for the town's prime (BlackPast). The first town marshal, Dick Shafer, was killed in a duel with a horse thief, who he in turn shot and killed (Washington, Outlook, 1908).

The town also reached for state institutions. Boley successfully bid to host the State Training School for Negro Boys and a Black tuberculosis hospital (BlackPast; the training school's site lineage is carried into Section 5 and flagged as single-sourced in the Research log).

Politics cut both ways. The same statehood that made Boley an Oklahoma town in 1907 brought Jim Crow law with it, and most of Boley's citizens were disfranchised in 1910 when the grandfather clause became law; the town became a center of the fight against disfranchisement for the next two decades (BlackPast). In October 1911, Boley hosted a State Convention of Colored Men and Women, organized by local clergymen, whose message to white neighbors distinguished law-abiding Black citizens from the criminal element and pledged cooperation against crime (Wikipedia, summarizing the scholarly literature).

3. Peak Years

By 1911, Boley counted more than four thousand citizens by the Oklahoma Historical Society's account, with two banks and three cotton gins (Oklahoma Historical Society; for the counting problem behind the 4,000 figure, see conflict 4). The business inventory preserved from the era runs to five grocery stores, five hotels, seven restaurants, four cotton gins, three drugstores, one jewelry store, four department stores, two livery stables, two insurance agencies, one undertaking establishment, one lumberyard, two photographers, and an ice plant (African American Registry, carrying the era's promotional record). The town's own Commercial Club published a booster pamphlet in 1911 titled "Facts about Boley Oklahoma: The Largest and Wealthiest Exclusive Negro City in the World" (cited in the Oklahoma Historical Society bibliography).

The endorsement that traveled farthest came in 1905, when Booker T. Washington visited and proclaimed Boley "the most enterprising and in many ways the most interesting of the Negro towns in the United States" (Oklahoma Historical Society). His written account followed in the Outlook of January 4, 1908. A longer version of the quotation, opening "Boley, Indian Territory, is the youngest, most enterprising," is carved on the town's commemorative plaque (Oklahoma Historical Society marker text; see conflict 8).

The peak was cultural as well as commercial. In 1922, rodeo legend Bill Pickett, credited as the inventor of bulldogging, shot his films The Bull-Dogger and The Crimson Skull at Boley (Wikipedia). Composer Zenobia Powell Perry was born in Boley in 1908, and baseball players Elijah "Pumpsie" Green, the first African American to play for the Boston Red Sox, and Cardell Camper were born there in 1933 and 1952 (Wikipedia, notable residents).

4. The Turning Point

Boley's turning point was economic and slow, and then, inside it, one violent morning that showed what the town was made of.

The slow turn: the town's wealth rode on cotton and the railroad. Farm prices fell from the late 1920s, and the Great Depression hit an agricultural town with full force; the population dropped to 1,154 in 1920 and 874 in 1930 (Oklahoma Historical Society; Boley Historic District summary). The railroad failed in stages. The Fort Smith and Western went bankrupt in 1915, emerged from reorganization in the early 1920s, and entered a new receivership in 1931 (Oklahoma Historical Society, Fort Smith and Western Railway entry). In 1939 the company was dissolved, its operations ceased on February 9, 1939, and the line through Boley was dismantled in 1939 and 1940 (Oklahoma Historical Society; Wikipedia). A railroad town had lost its railroad.

The violent morning: on November 23, 1932, three members of Pretty Boy Floyd's gang, George Birdwell, C. C. Patterson, and Charles "Pete" Glass, attempted to rob the Farmers and Merchants Bank. Floyd himself had warned his men not to attack Boley because its residents were well armed (News9, Oklahoma City; Wikipedia). Inside the bank, president David J. Turner sounded the alarm; Birdwell shot and killed him. Assistant cashier Herbert C. McCormick, who had taken position in the vault with a rifle, shot Birdwell, who collapsed and died in the bank. Outside, armed townspeople met Patterson as he fled; though shot repeatedly, he survived and was arrested by Sheriff Joseph Langston McCormick, the cashier's brother. Glass, the driver, was shot dead as he tried to drive away (Wikipedia, assembling the sourced accounts; News9). The robbery failed completely. Floyd reportedly vowed revenge and never returned; he was killed by law enforcement in Ohio in October 1934 (town histories). The exact sequence of shots inside the bank differs between accounts; see the Research log open questions. Told plainly, the episode is the documentary center of Boley's self-image: a Black town that defended its own bank at the cost of its bank president's life.

5. Survival, Decline, or Destruction

Boley declined for half a century and did not die. The census arc tells the long version: 942 in 1940, 646 in 1950, 573 in 1960, 514 in 1970, and a low of 423 in 1980 (Oklahoma Historical Society; U.S. decennial census table). The town remained incorporated and self-governing throughout, which is the fact that separates Boley from most of the fifty-plus Oklahoma Black towns: it is one of only thirteen still existing (Oklahoma Historical Society).

Recognition arrived while the town was at its smallest. On May 15, 1975, the Boley Historic District was listed in the National Register of Historic Places and designated a National Historic Landmark (National Park Service, NR 75001568). The district covers 34.5 acres, roughly bounded by Seward Avenue, Walnut and Cedar Streets, and the southern city limits, and includes 14 contributing buildings or structures, all built between 1903 and 1921 (Boley Historic District summary, from the National Register record).

The population figures rebound after 1980 for a reason that is not a civilian revival alone. In 1983, the John H. Lilley Correctional Center opened in Boley, a state minimum-security prison (Oklahoma CareerTech; Oklahoma Department of Corrections). The facility stands in a lineage that begins with the State Training School for Negro Boys, moved to Boley in the early twentieth century (facility histories; BlackPast records the town's successful bid for the school). The census counted 908 residents in 1990, 1,126 in 2000, and 1,184 in 2010 (U.S. decennial census), totals that include the prison's roughly 800 inmates (Oklahoma Department of Corrections fact sheet, August 2025: more than 800 male inmates).

Preservation work continues on the bank building at the center of the 1932 story: Oklahoma news coverage reported a National Park Service grant of $24,000 toward its restoration, with the Coltrane Group leading the project (News9).

6. The Town Today

Boley today is a working incorporated town on U.S. Highway 62 in Okfuskee County, at 35.4956 N, 96.4828 W (National Register district coordinates), with an active mayor, clerk, and Chamber of Commerce and an official website, cityofboley.org (project outreach plan, verified October 2026). The 2020 census counted 1,091 residents (U.S. decennial census; see conflict 5 on the Historical Society's 1,087 figure, and Section 5 on the prison's share of that count).

What a visitor finds: the Boley Historic District downtown, whose contributing buildings include the Farmers and Merchants Bank building and the Antioch Baptist Church, originally built in 1903, the town's founding year (Wikipedia, district and town articles); the Oklahoma Historical Society markers on U.S. 62, including the founding marker carrying the May 11, 1905 incorporation date and the 1975 landmark designation; and a marker on the Oklahoma Civil Rights Trail, reported as installed in 2024 (historic Black Wall Street project site, a secondary source; flagged in the Research log). Boley High School closed in 2007 and the elementary school in 2010 (Wikipedia timeline).

The town's signature gathering is the Boley Rodeo and Bar-B-Que Festival, held every Memorial Day weekend. The Oklahoma Historical Society calls it the nation's oldest African American community-based rodeo, and town tradition dates it to 1903, the founding year (Wikipedia; see conflict 10). The 2026 edition drew an estimated 12,000 to 15,000 people (project outreach plan). A local manufacturer, Smokarama Inc., maker of pressure smokers, is reported as a Boley business (BlackAmericaWeb; single source, flagged in the Research log).

Lyric annotations, line by line

Lyric annotation (written 2026-10-26 from the archived summary and upgraded the same day against the full verbatim lyric text; each entry ties the lyric content to this dossier's verified record, and compressions are named rather than hidden):

  • Opening, "Oklahoma, 1903" (archived excerpt): the year is right and the state is an anachronism. In 1903 the townsite stood in the Creek Nation, Indian Territory; Oklahoma statehood came in 1907. The song opens in the present-day map's language, a standard compression.
  • "Creek freedmen with land deeds": the land base is documented. The townsite stood on Abigail Barnett's 160-acre Creek freedmen allotment, inherited from her father James Barnett (National Register nomination). "Deeds" compresses the allotment system, under which the land was held by federal allotment rather than ordinary purchase.
  • "Miss Abigail": verse 1 has Miss Abigail planting prayers in the field with her hands in the earth, saying "freedom is real." The name matches the documented allottee, Abigail Barnett, the Creek freedwoman whose 160-acre allotment carried the townsite (Section 1; National Register nomination). The lyric recasts her as a field-working founding mother; the documentary Barnett is the landholder whose allotment was leased for the townsite, and no source consulted in this pass shows her planting the town's fields. Poetic license on a real name, named here rather than read as a documented scene.
  • "Electric currents in the veins of the town": the image rests on a documented fact. Boley owned its own electrical generating plant, with its water system and ice plant, and is widely described as home to the first Black-owned electric company (Section 2; Oklahoma Historical Society; African American Registry). The lyric sets the image inside its 1903 founding tableau; the sources consulted do not date the plant's construction, so the substance is verified and the founding-year placement is not.
  • "D.J. Turner's vision": David J. Turner is the song's central figure, and he was central in fact: bank co-founder in 1906, repeated mayor and councilman, pharmacist, and the bank president killed defending the Farmers and Merchants Bank in 1932 (Kansas City Fed; Sections 2 and 4). But he was an early settler and builder, not the founder; the founding vision in the documents belongs to townsite manager T. M. Haynes and the promoting group (Section 1). The song's choice is a defensible dramatic focus, named here as a compression.
  • "Two banks": verified for the town's first years (Farmers and Merchants Bank, organized 1906; Boley Bank and Trust, founded 1907), with the First National Bank of Boley following in 1921 (Kansas City Fed). The bridge names one bank outright: "Farmers and Teachers State Bank where dreams got paid." That name is the lyric's own, confirmed against the full text; no source consulted records a Boley bank by that name, and the documented name is Farmers and Merchants. See conflict 7.
  • "Boley Hotel where the jazz bands played": the bridge's line, verbatim. Boley's hotels are documented (five in the era inventory carried by the African American Registry; Washington noted a hotel in 1908), and Pickett's 1922 filming shows the town on the entertainment circuit, but a hotel by the specific name Boley Hotel, and its jazz scene, were not verified in this pass. Annotated as plausible period color, not established fact.
  • "Masonic Lodge": verified and understated. The Grand Lodge's Masonic Temple, completed around 1912, was said to be the tallest building between Okmulgee and Oklahoma City (Oklahoma Historical Society).
  • "African immigrants claimin' their promised land": the bridge's migration image is not documented. The settler record in this pass is internal migration: formerly enslaved people and their children from Georgia, Texas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, and Florida, drawn by Haynes's advertising (Section 1; National Register nomination). No source consulted documents African immigrant settlers at Boley's founding. Carried as a lyric flourish, flagged here.
  • "Most all-Black town that the world ever seen": the bridge's superlative echoes Boley's own era booster language. The town's 1911 Commercial Club pamphlet billed Boley as "The Largest and Wealthiest Exclusive Negro City in the World" (Section 3), and the Historical Society calls Boley the largest and best known of Oklahoma's all-Black towns. As a world claim it is booster rhetoric in both centuries; within Oklahoma's Black towns, the record supports it.
  • "Black doctors, lawyers, newspapers": the newspaper is documented by name (the Boley Progress, from 1905). The professional class is consistent with the documented record (Turner himself practiced pharmacy), but no doctor or lawyer is named in the sources consulted in this pass; annotated as consistent with the record, not separately verified.
  • "Cotton gins": verified (two by Washington's 1908 account; three by 1911 per the Oklahoma Historical Society; four in the era inventory; Hilliard Taylor's ginning business).
  • "Oil wells": partially supported at the county level only. An oil discovery near Okemah brought the Fort Smith and Western real traffic and postponed the railroad's abandonment (Oklahoma Historical Society; Wikipedia). No source consulted in this pass documents oil as an industry inside Boley itself; annotated as a county-level truth carried into the town's story.
  • "Black Wall Street before Tulsa": a framing phrase, not a documented period nickname. The chronology it claims is real (Boley's peak began with the 1903 founding and its 1911 boosters called it "the largest and wealthiest exclusive Negro city in the world," two decades before Tulsa's Greenwood became famous), but no source in this pass shows contemporaries calling Boley a Black Wall Street. See conflict 12.
  • "Jim Crow pressure and dried-up oil caused decline": half documented. Jim Crow pressure is documented (statehood-era segregation, the 1910 grandfather clause; BlackPast). "Dried-up oil" is not documented as a cause of Boley's decline in any source consulted; the documented causes are the farm-price collapse from the late 1920s, the Great Depression, and the railroad's abandonment in 1939 (Section 4). See conflict 12.
  • "The spirit endures" (the outro's closing judgment, "Boley still breathin'"): thematic, and the record supports the sentiment: the town never disincorporated, holds its landmark district, and still hosts the rodeo (Sections 5 and 6).

Boley also appears as a named example in the catalog's overview song "Land Lines: The Homestead Cipher" (batch 2b), a Homestead Act survey piece; the original "Boley, Oklahoma" is the town's own song, and the one this dossier annotates. Its May 2026 remaster carries the title "IRON TRACKS TO BOLEY (Remastered)."

Conflicts between sources, recorded not smoothed

  1. Three founding dates for three different events. Townsite established August 1903 (Oklahoma Historical Society marker); town opened for settlement September 26, 1903 (town history; the National Register nomination dates the settlement's start to the 1903 railroad camp); formal opening held September 22, 1904 (National Register nomination; the African American Registry calls September 22, 1904 the date the town was "formally established"). The dossier carries all three with their events named, and the Key Facts Box leads with 1903.
  2. Abigail's name. The Oklahoma Historical Society encyclopedia and the National Register nomination give Abigail Barnett, the nomination adding her married name: Abigail Barnett McCormick. The African American Registry and a BlackAmericaWeb account spell the family name "Burnett" ("Abigail Burnett McCormick"). The dossier carries Barnett, per the nomination and the Historical Society, and flags the Burnett variant.
  3. Who was the first mayor. The sources document T. M. Haynes as townsite manager (National Register nomination; Historical Society marker), and the African American Registry says he also served as the town's first mayor. A popular web account (BlackAmericaWeb) says Abigail Barnett was "named mayor of the town," a claim no authoritative source consulted repeats. David J. Turner's repeated terms as mayor are documented in the town history. The dossier carries Haynes as townsite manager and principal founder and does not name a first mayor as established; the question stays open in the Research log.
  4. Peak population. More than 4,000 citizens by 1911 (Oklahoma Historical Society), with the marker adding 25,000 in the surrounding area; the town plaque says the population was "boosted to 4,200"; Washington reported about 2,500 inhabitants in 1908; the Historical Society counts 824 inside the town at statehood in 1907; and the incorporated town's highest decennial census count is 1,334 (1910). These figures cannot all describe the same boundary. The 4,000-plus numbers are consistent with counting the town plus its farm trade territory, and the largest of them come from a booster context (the 1911 Commercial Club pamphlet). The Key Facts Box marks peak population NOT ESTABLISHED and carries each figure with its basis.
  5. The 2020 census count. The decennial census table gives 1,091 (the figure the project's outreach plan also carries); the Oklahoma Historical Society encyclopedia says the April 2020 census counted 1,087 residents. The dossier carries 1,091 and flags the Historical Society's 1,087.
  6. Which bank held the national charter. The Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City's banking history is precise: the First National Bank of Boley, opened in 1921, was the first Black-owned bank with a national charter. A Wikipedia photograph caption and several popular retellings attach the same distinction to the Farmers and Merchants Bank (organized 1906 as a state bank). The dossier follows the Kansas City Fed and flags the popular version.
  7. The song's bank name. The bridge of the full lyric text names one of Boley's banks the "Farmers and Teachers State Bank." Settled against the verbatim lyrics on 2026-10-26: the name is in the lyric itself, not an archive transcription slip. No source consulted records a Boley bank by that name. The documented names are the Farmers and Merchants Bank (1906), Boley Bank and Trust (1907 to 1910), and the First National Bank of Boley (1921). The lyric name is carried as the lyricist's compression of Farmers and Merchants and flagged here.
  8. Booker T. Washington: visit and words. The Historical Society dates his visit to 1905, and Washington's own account opens "In 1905, when I visited Indian Territory," but the African American Registry ties his famous declaration to the 1904 formal opening. The quotation itself varies: the Historical Society's version runs "the most enterprising and in many ways the most interesting of the Negro towns in the United States," while the town plaque's version adds "youngest" at the front ("Boley, Indian Territory, is the youngest, most enterprising..."). The dossier follows the Historical Society on the 1905 visit and quotes its version, flagging both variants.
  9. Two banks by 1911. The Historical Society says Boley boasted two banks by 1911, but the Kansas City Fed's chronology has Boley Bank and Trust merging into the Farmers and Merchants Bank in early 1910, leaving one bank until the First National Bank of Boley opened in 1921. A second institution in 1911 is not identified by either source. Unresolved in this pass; the dossier presents the bank timeline from the Kansas City Fed and records the discrepancy here.
  10. The rodeo's start. Town tradition and the Wikipedia town article date the Boley Rodeo to 1903, the founding year, and call it the longest-running Black rodeo in the United States; the Historical Society calls it the nation's oldest African American community-based rodeo, held every Memorial Day weekend. A timeline mirror (Alchetron) instead dates the first annual Boley Rodeo and Bar-B-Que Festival to 1961. The likely reconciliation, an older rodeo tradition formalized as an annual festival later, is not stated by any source consulted and is not assumed here. The dossier carries the tradition date with its sources and flags the 1961 figure.
  11. The caption's founding compression. The song's caption (the user's own writing) says Boley was "Founded in 1903 by Creek Freedmen." The documented founding group is interracial (Haynes, Moore, J. B. Boley) working with Creek freedman James Barnett's family and building on his daughter's allotment (National Register nomination; BlackPast). The caption's version honors the land's origin and the town's Black self-governance while erasing the townsite promotion structure. Quoted as-is in Section 7; flagged here rather than silently corrected.
  12. The song's decline account. The full lyric text attributes decline to Jim Crow ("Jim Crow came knockin' with federal schemes") and dried-up oil ("Oil dried up, folks moved away"). Jim Crow is documented (BlackPast); dried-up oil is not documented as a cause in any source consulted, and oil itself is documented only at the county level (the Okemah discovery and railroad traffic). The documented decline causes are the farm-price collapse of the late 1920s, the Great Depression, and the Fort Smith and Western's 1939 abandonment (Sections 4 and 9). The related phrase "Black Wall Street before Tulsa" is a modern framing, not a period nickname found in this pass. Both are carried in the annotation as compressions.
