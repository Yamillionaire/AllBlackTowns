Key Facts Box

  • Founded: 1738. Governor Manuel de Montiano of Spanish Florida granted freedom to Francisco Menendez and the other freedom seekers who had been re-enslaved in Florida, on March 15, 1738 in the National Park Service women history account, and established their town that year (National Park Service, Fort Mose and Castillo de San Marcos pages; Florida State Parks, History of Fort Mose; Archaeology Magazine, March-April 2024). Florida Memory dates the establishment to 1739; see conflict 1.
  • Founded by: the freedom seekers themselves, self-emancipated Africans who had escaped enslavement in the English colonies of Carolina (and later Georgia) and reached Spanish St. Augustine, organized under their militia captain Francisco Menendez, with the legal sanction and land grant of Governor Manuel de Montiano (National Park Service; Florida Museum of Natural History; Jane Landers, Black Society in Spanish Florida, 1999). The formal Spanish name was Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose, in English Royal Grace of Saint Teresa of Mose (National Park Service; National Historic Landmark nomination).
  • Incorporated: never incorporated as a United States municipality. Fort Mose was a Spanish colonial town and fortified settlement, chartered by the governor of Spanish Florida in 1738, and it ceased to exist as a community when its people evacuated to Cuba in 1763, two generations before Florida became United States territory (National Park Service; Florida State Parks).
  • Peak population: NOT ESTABLISHED as a single documented peak. The estimates and counts divide by date and by what they count, and this dossier carries each with its basis rather than picking one: about 100 people at the first settlement (38 men and their families) in National Park Service and Florida State Parks estimates; 67 people in the documented 1759 census of the second settlement (37 men, 15 women, 7 boys, and 8 girls in 22 houses); and 87 souls in the colony at the 1763 evacuation in one scholarly abstract. See Section 4 and conflict 3.
  • Population today: none. Fort Mose has no resident population. Its site is a state park with a visitor center and museum, grounds, and, since May 9, 2025, a full-scale reconstruction of the 1738 fort (Florida State Parks; St. Johns County, May 13, 2025).
  • Known for: the first legally sanctioned free Black settlement in what is now the United States; a town founded by people who freed themselves, conditioned on Catholic conversion and militia service; the free Black militia under Captain Francisco Menendez and the Battle of Bloody Mose in June 1740; and a southern route to freedom that historian Jane Landers calls the first underground railroad, running south, not north (National Park Service; Florida State Parks; Landers, quoted in Archaeology Magazine, 2024).
  • The song: "Fort Mose, Florida," the redo built on the 2026-11-05 script, produced by the user from that script, mixed, and finished to a master on 2026-11-09; it supersedes "Gracia's Promise" (June 19, 2025, 2:51). See Section 8.

Introduction

  • Town name, State: Fort Mose, Florida
  • County: St. Johns County
  • Status: historic site. No town stands today. The site of the second fort (1752-1763), on a marsh island on Robinson Creek (the Mose Creek of the colonial record) about two miles north of St. Augustine, is preserved as Fort Mose Historic State Park, a 40-acre Florida state park at 15 Fort Mose Trail, St. Augustine, and was designated a National Historic Landmark on October 12, 1994 (National Park Service; Florida State Parks; National Register record NR 94001645). The site of the first fort (1738-1740) has not been definitively located; see conflict 7.

Timeline: the story at a glance

1565Pedro Menendez de Aviles founds St. Augustine, the Spanish capital of Florida; free and enslaved Africans are present in the colony from its first years (National Park Service, Castillo de San Marcos).
1687the first documented freedom seekers from the English colonies reach St. Augustine: eight men, two women, and a young child arrive by boat from Carolina (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; the National Park Service Gullah Geechee Corridor account dates the first reported runaways to 1687). Spanish officials report runaways arriving from this period forward.
1680sthe Spanish organize an African militia unit in St. Augustine (Florida Memory, State Library of Florida).
November 7, 1693King Charles II of Spain issues the royal edict, the first proclamation, granting liberty to freedom seekers from the English colonies who reached Florida, in the standard translation "giving liberty to all" (National Park Service, Gullah Geechee Corridor; Florida Memory; Archaeology Magazine, 2024). Later accounts attach the conditions of Catholic conversion and, for men, a term of militia service; see Section 1 and conflict 5.
1715Francisco Menendez, a Mandinga man from the Gambia region of West Africa enslaved in South Carolina, escapes during the Yamasee War, fights alongside the Yamasee, and travels south to St. Augustine; he is claimed by the Yamasee leader Perro Bravo, purchased by the acting governor, and sold at auction to the royal accountant Francisco Menendez Marquez, whose name he takes at Catholic baptism (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Jane Landers, Menendez biography, African American National Biography).
1726Governor Antonio de Benavides appoints Francisco Menendez commander of a newly formed militia of formerly enslaved people; the unit helps defend St. Augustine against an English attack in 1728 (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).
March 15, 1738Governor Manuel de Montiano grants unconditional freedom to Menendez and the other re-enslaved freedom seekers, on the National Park Service dating, and establishes Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose about two miles north of St. Augustine, with Menendez as its civil and military leader (National Park Service, women history page; Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Landers biography). More than 100 escapees had reached St. Augustine by this year (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Florida Museum).
1739Florida Memory dates the establishment of the settlement to this year, and the National Park Service corridor account says fugitive slaves inhabited Fort Mose as early as 1739; see conflict 1.
December 28, 1739Francisco Menendez and Ana Maria de Escovar are married; the parish registry records both as Mandingas who had belonged to the royal accountant (Landers biography).
September 1739the Stono Rebellion breaks out near the Stono River in South Carolina; the English blame the Spanish promise of freedom in part, and the War of Jenkins Ear between Britain and Spain follows (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).
May-June 1740General James Oglethorpe of Georgia besieges St. Augustine; the people of Mose evacuate to the city, British and Scottish troops with Native allies occupy the empty fort, and at dawn on June 26, 1740 by modern (New Style) dating, June 15 by the contemporary (Old Style) dating in Florida Memory, a combined force of Spanish regulars, the Mose militia under Menendez, and Native auxiliaries retakes the fort in the battle the British call Bloody Mose; the fort is destroyed in the fighting (Florida Memory, The British Invasion; Florida Historical Society, Florida Frontiers; National Park Service). See Section 5 and conflict 2.
December 12, 1740Menendez petitions the king of Spain for recognition and recompense for his valor in the 1740 fighting, in the first of his merits and services letters (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).
July 1741Menendez, sailing on a Spanish corsair ship in hope of reaching Spain to press his case, is captured by the British, taken to the Bahamas, and ordered sold into slavery by an admiralty court; by 1743 he is free and back in St. Augustine, by a route the record does not establish (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Landers).
1752Governor Fulgencio Garcia de Solis orders the Black community of St. Augustine to rebuild and reoccupy Fort Mose at a new site a few hundred yards north of the first fort; the relocation is forced and is resisted by militiamen who had put down roots in the city (National Park Service; Archaeology Magazine, 2024).
1759Father Juan Joseph de Solana describes the second fort; an artillery inventory of April 20, 1759 lists two iron cannons, four swivel guns, and 24 muskets; the 1759 census counts 67 residents in 22 houses (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Florida Museum historical archaeology page).
1763the Treaty of Paris (the Peace of Paris) ends the French and Indian War, the North American theater of the Seven Years War, and cedes Florida to Britain; the people of Mose evacuate to Cuba with the rest of Spanish St. Augustine rather than live under British rule (National Park Service; Florida Memory).
1764the Mose families, after a period in Regla near Havana, are among the Florida evacuees settled at San Agustin de la Nueva Florida, at Ceiba Mocha in Matanzas Province, Cuba, on land donated in 1764 (Landers, An Eighteenth-Century Community in Exile: The Floridanos in Cuba, 1996; Archaeology Magazine, 2024).
1783-1784Spain regains Florida; the British-era fort is refurbished and used again as a Spanish outpost; some Africans from the evacuation era return to Florida in the Second Spanish period, though Menendez, on Landers research, does not (Florida Memory; Archaeology Magazine, 2024).
1790Spain, under pressure from the new United States government, ends the sanctuary policy (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).
1812Fort Mose, by then a Spanish military outpost, is burned during the Patriot War when a Spanish schooner fires on it to dislodge its American occupiers (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Black Catholic Messenger summary).
1880srailroad and hotel builder Henry Flagler dredges the Mose area for fill, reshaping it into the salt marsh and islands seen today; ruins of the second fort were still visible as late as the 1850s (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).
1971limited archaeological testing touches the site (National Historic Landmark nomination; Davidson et al., 2020 abstract, which dates archaeological rediscovery to 1971).
1986-1988the Fort Mose Research Team, led by archaeologist Kathleen Deagan of the Florida Museum of Natural History as principal investigator, with historian Jane Landers and John Marron, excavates and definitively identifies the site of the second fort (1752-1763) (National Park Service; Wikipedia cross-check of the research team record; Archaeology Magazine, 2024).
1989the State of Florida acquires the site; one visiting account gives 41 acres purchased in 1989 with further acquisitions in 1998 and 2004, against the park present 40-acre description; see Section 7 (VISIT FLORIDA; Florida State Parks).
October 12, 1994Fort Mose is designated a National Historic Landmark and listed in the National Register of Historic Places, reference number 94001645 (National Register record; Florida State Parks). One Florida Museum page gives 1995; see conflict 4.
1996the Fort Mose Historical Society is founded by leaders of the St. Augustine Black community, Charles Ellis among its founders (Wikipedia cross-check; EIN Presswire, May 14, 2025).
2007the state park visitor center is completed, on the Omeka bibliography account (St. Augustine bibliography page).
2009the National Park Service names Fort Mose a precursor site on the National Underground Railroad Network to Freedom (Florida Historic Coast release, 2023; National Park Service).
2019UNESCO lists Fort Mose as a Site of Memory of the Slave Route Project, on the 1st Florida county history account (see Section 10); new archaeological investigations by Flagler College, the University of Florida, the University of Texas at Austin, and the Lighthouse Archaeological Maritime Program begin and run to 2024 (Wikipedia cross-check).
January 19, 2024groundbreaking for the full-scale reconstruction of the 1738 fort (St. Johns County, May 13, 2025).
May 9, 2025the reconstructed 1738 fort, more than 39 feet tall with its moat, opens to the public in a ribbon-cutting at Fort Mose Historic State Park; final cost 3.2 million dollars from public and private sources (St. Johns County, May 13, 2025; Florida State Parks Foundation release).
November 5, 2025the user rules that Fort Mose is pronounced "Mo-say" and directs the song redo; the redo script is prepared the same day (project file fort-mose-redo-script-2026-11-05.md).
November 9, 2026the redo song "Fort Mose, Florida" is finished to a master, on the assignment record for this dossier; the redo script file itself is dated 2026-11-05. See Section 8.

The Song

Fort Mose, two miles north of St. Augustine, Florida, was established in 1738 as the first legally sanctioned free Black settlement in what is now the United States. Freedom seekers who escaped slavery in Carolina won their liberty under Spain's offer, and their militia, led by Captain Francisco Menéndez, defended the settlement. No town stands today; the site is Fort Mose Historic State Park, a National Historic Landmark.

The style, in summary: a stripped-down skeleton groove, crisp woodblocks, snapped snares, and minimal metallic stabs over a heavyweight Bay Area mobb bassline, slow and deliberate, fronted by an alto Black female rapper alternating warm conversational flow with hard punchlines, with vinyl hiss and talkbox flourishes (archive style prompt, paraphrased here in brief; the full prompt is preserved verbatim in the redo script file record section).

The caption, paraphrased to keep this file free of the original em-dash punctuation (the original caption is preserved verbatim in the redo script file, and this dossier paraphrases rather than quotes it, a choice stated here per the project rule): the caption gives Fort Mose dates as 1738-1763, places it north of St. Augustine, calls it the first legally sanctioned free Black settlement in what is now the United States, says it was founded by escaped enslaved Africans who converted to Catholicism and fought in the Spanish militia, says it defended Spanish Florida most famously during the 1740 Bloody Mose battle, says Britain regained control in 1763 and residents relocated, and closes by calling Fort Mose today a National Historic Landmark and a symbol of early Black autonomy (archive caption, paraphrased). Every element of that caption is verified by this dossier record, with two refinements the dossier adds: the 1763 relocation destination was Cuba (Section 6), and the caption "relocated" is the whole of what the old song record claimed.

The lyric summary in the archive: the song is set in 1738 at the "Gracia Real" fortress; it carries the Spanish crown offer (convert, enlist, shackles undone), Captain Menendez leading freedmen soldiers, refugees from Carolina trading bondage for muskets, Catholic hymns and sacred ground built brick by brick; its hook carries the line "freedom rose from Spanish sand" and the fragment "Mose was more than just a gate, it was a war cry"; its second verse carries a sovereign Black holy space before Emancipation, lost to treaties and time; its outro carries "Before Harriet, before the Proclamation, there was Gracia's Promise, freedom's foundation" in the archive rendering (archive lyrics summary). Annotation of the superseded song, against this dossier record: its frame is accurate (1738, Gracia Real as the short form of the verified full name, the crown terms, Menendez, the Carolina origin, the treaty loss). Its one naming limit is the reason the redo exists, and it is a pronunciation limit, not a factual one: the song sang the town name in a way the user ruled wrong (see the redo below). The redo keeps this song architecture deliberately: Verse 1, Hook, Verse 2, Hook, Outro (redo script file).

Authorship framing, per the project standing record, for both songs: the lyrics are human-written; the music and arrangement are the project's own.

Line-by-line annotation of the display-copy lyrics against the verified record of Sections 1 to 7. Verdicts use four labels: VERIFIED (the record supports the line as stated), SUPPORTED WITH NUANCE (the substance is documented, the phrasing compresses it), POETIC (image or rhetoric, no factual claim to check), and CORRECTED HERE (the line states as fact something the record divides on or contradicts, and the correction stands in this annotation and in the conflicts list; the lyric itself is the song of record and is not rewritten by this dossier).

Verse 1:

  • "Seventeen thirty-eight, north of St. Augustine town" : VERIFIED. Founding year 1738 and position north of St. Augustine (Sections 1 and 2; Florida State Parks).
  • "Word came through the swamp: Spain is laying bondage down" : SUPPORTED WITH NUANCE. Word of Spanish sanctuary did spread to the Carolina plantations and drew escapees from 1687 forward; "laying bondage down" compresses a conditional sanctuary (conversion and service) into an abolition Spain never declared for its own colony, where slavery continued (Sections 1 and 2; Florida Memory notes not all Black people in Florida obtained freedom).
  • "Leave the Carolina chain, cross the water, cross the pine" : VERIFIED in substance. The freedom seekers came from Carolina, by boat in the first documented 1687 arrival and overland through marsh and pine country thereafter (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).
  • "Kneel to the Catholic faith, and the crown will draw the line" : VERIFIED. Catholic conversion was a stated condition of freedom in every account (Section 1).
  • "Freedom for the ones who flee, service in the militia band" : VERIFIED. Militia service was the second condition (Section 1; conflict 5 on the four-year term variant).
  • "Shackles undone by royal word, freedom written in the sand" : VERIFIED as to the royal word: the 1693 edict of Charles II, enforced for the petitioners by Montiano in 1738 (Section 1). The sand is coastal image, POETIC.
  • "So they ran, whole families, through the marsh in the night" : SUPPORTED WITH NUANCE. Families, including women and children, are documented from the 1687 arrival forward, and 38 households formed the first town; the night and marsh detail is scene-setting no source specifies (Sections 1 and 2).
  • "Traded bondage for a musket and a town built in the light" : VERIFIED in substance, following the archive summary own phrase for the superseded song, "trading bondage for muskets" (archive summary; Sections 1 and 2).
  • "Gracia Real, that is what the Spanish named the ground" : VERIFIED as the short form. The full formal name is Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose, verified in this dossier research and withheld from the redo script only because the project record then held the short form (Section 1; redo script file, deliberately not used list). The song short form is accurate as far as it goes.
  • "The first free Black settlement any law had ever crowned" : VERIFIED, in the sources own terms: first legally sanctioned free Black settlement in what is now the United States (Key Facts Box).
  • "Captain Menendez stood tall where the free men stand" : VERIFIED as to the man and rank, with the dossier research supplying what the script withheld: his first name is Francisco, verified across the National Park Service, Landers biography, and Archaeology Magazine (Section 3). The script sings only "Captain Menendez" because the project record then held only that form (redo script file).
  • "A captain of freedmen, with the militia in his hand" : VERIFIED. Menendez commanded the free Black militia from 1726 and led the Mose militia and town (Section 3).
  • "They raised a fort of earth and timber, raised a church to pray" : VERIFIED. Earth and log (timber) fort; a church stood in both forts (Sections 2).
  • "Hymns in the morning, and a watch kept through the day" : SUPPORTED WITH NUANCE. Catholic worship with a resident priest and militia guard and patrol duty are both documented; the morning hymn schedule is scene detail (Sections 2 and 4; Florida Museum).
  • "Every child born on that ground was born free" : SUPPORTED WITH NUANCE. Children of the free Mose families were free, and the censuses count boys and girls born into the town free households; no source consulted states the universal in the lyric form, and the line is carried as consistent with the record (Section 4).
  • "Fort Mose, first free town, and the whole world would see" : the first clause VERIFIED; the second is POETIC address.

Hook (sung twice; annotated once):

  • "Freedom rose from Spanish sand" : preserved fragment from the superseded song (archive summary); POETIC image on a VERIFIED event.
  • "First free town built by free Black hands" : VERIFIED. The residents built their own fort, church, and homes (Sections 2 and 3).
  • "Mose was more than just a gate, it was a war cry" : preserved fragment from the superseded song (archive summary); POETIC, with a factual root: Mose was a town, not only a defensive gate for St. Augustine, and its name did become a battle name in 1740 (Section 5).
  • "Black and free beneath a Spanish sky" : VERIFIED as the town legal condition (Sections 1 and 2).
  • "Fort Mose, say the name and say it proud" : POETIC address; the name teaching is the redo stated purpose (redo script file).
  • "First free ground, and the ground still speaks loud" : the first clause VERIFIED; the second POETIC, with a documentary echo: the ground did speak again through the 1986-1988 excavations (Section 7).
  • "Fort Mose, seventeen thirty-eight" : VERIFIED date.
  • "Freedom did not have to wait" : POETIC summary of the song thesis, consistent with the record that Mose freedom predates general emancipation by 125 years (see Verse 2 annotation).

Verse 2:

  • "They farmed that Florida ground and they governed their own" : SUPPORTED WITH NUANCE. Farming is documented, though the crops never fed the town fully (Section 2). Self-government is documented in the form of Menendez civil leadership under Spanish authority, not independence from it; the line is accurate about internal governance and silent about the Spanish oversight above it (Section 2).
  • "Black families, Black militia, Black church, Black home" : VERIFIED as the town composition (Sections 2 to 4).
  • "Wives and husbands, elders, children, a people entire" : VERIFIED in the census shape: most households were married couples and children; the 1759 count names men, women, boys, and girls (Section 4).
  • "With a fort for a shield and a church bell for a spire" : the fort VERIFIED; the bell and spire are POETIC. A wooden church is documented in both forts; no source consulted documents a bell or a spire.
  • "Seventeen forty, the British came down for the fort" : VERIFIED. Oglethorpe invasion, 1740 (Section 5).
  • "Bloody Mose, Bloody Mose, the name that the battle bore" : VERIFIED. Bloody Mose is the British name for the June 1740 retaking (Section 5).
  • "Menendez and his militia would not yield an inch of ground" : CORRECTED HERE in part. The documented sequence is an ordered evacuation to St. Augustine, a British occupation, and then a dawn retaking by Spanish regulars, the Mose militia, and Native auxiliaries together; the militia did not hold the fort in place, and it did not retake it alone (Section 5). The line carries the refusal to surrender the town cause, which the retaking proves, and compresses the sequence and the allied force out of the picture.
  • "They defended Spanish Florida, and Mose still stood sound" : the defense VERIFIED; "still stood sound" CORRECTED HERE. The fort was destroyed in the battle and the townsite was abandoned for twelve years; what stood was the community, in St. Augustine, not the fort (Sections 5 and 6). The superseded caption own claim, that Mose "defended Spanish Florida," is the verified half of this line.
  • "A hundred twenty-five years before the Proclamation's call" : VERIFIED arithmetic and framing: 1738 to the Emancipation Proclamation of 1863 is 125 years; the superseded song Verse 2 and Outro used the same frame (archive summary).
  • "Freedom was already standing, and Mose was standing tall" : POETIC summary on the verified chronology.
  • "Seventeen sixty-three, a treaty changed the shore" : VERIFIED. The Treaty of Paris, 1763 (Section 6).
  • "Britain held Florida, and Spain held it no more" : VERIFIED for 1763-1783; Spain regained Florida in 1783, after the Mose community had left, a sequel the song does not claim (Section 6; Timeline).
  • "And the people of Mose would not live as slaves again" : SUPPORTED WITH NUANCE. The evacuation to Cuba preserved their freedom and the record frames the move as accompanying the Spaniards rather than facing British rule; the motive stated in the line is the standard scholarly framing and is consistent with the choice documented, though no Mose-authored statement of motive was consulted in this pass (Section 6).
  • "They relocated, free, every woman, child, and man" : VERIFIED, with the dossier research supplying the destination the script withheld: Cuba, then Regla, then San Agustin de la Nueva Florida at Ceiba Mocha, Matanzas Province (Section 6). "Every" is supported for the Mose colony as a body by the 87-souls evacuation count; individual exceptions, if any, are not documented in the sources consulted.
  • "The town fell silent, but the ground kept the claim" : POETIC, on verified facts: abandonment after 1763 and archaeological survival of the second fort site (Sections 6 and 7).

1. Founding

Fort Mose was founded by people who had already freed themselves, and the founding begins fifty years before the town, with the road they opened.

Enslaved Africans began escaping from the English plantations of Carolina to Spanish St. Augustine in the late 1600s. Archaeology Magazine, reporting Jane Landers documentary research, dates the first documented arrival to 1687, when eight men, two women, and a young child arrived by boat from the Carolinas (Archaeology Magazine, March-April 2024). The National Park Service Gullah Geechee Corridor account agrees on the year: Spanish officials reported the first runaways from the English settlements in 1687 (National Park Service). Florida Memory adds the institutional frame: in the 1680s the Spanish organized an African militia unit in St. Augustine, and the newcomers entered a colony where free and enslaved Africans already lived alongside Spaniards (Florida Memory; National Park Service, Castillo de San Marcos).

On November 7, 1693, King Charles II of Spain formalized the welcome in a royal edict, the first proclamation, granting liberty to freedom seekers from the English colonies. The edict language carried in the scholarly translation is "giving liberty to all... men as well as the women... so that by their example, and by my liberty others will do the same" (edict translation by Bruce Twyman, carried in the Siege of Fort Mose summary; National Park Service corridor account). Florida Memory states the same core: in 1693 the king established legal sanctuary for runaway slaves who reached Florida (Florida Memory). The conditions attached to that freedom are stated with small differences across the sources and are recorded as conflict 5: Catholic conversion is constant in every account; militia service, in some accounts a four-year term for men, is the second condition (National Park Service; Florida State Parks; Florida Memory). What is not in dispute is the bargain as the town lived it: freedom in Spanish Florida required allegiance to the king of Spain, membership in the Catholic Church, and, for the men, service in the militia (Florida State Parks, History of Fort Mose).

The policy was also strategy, and the sources say so plainly. By offering freedom, Spain struck an economic blow against the English colonies, depriving them of labor, and gained defenders who, in Landers words as reported by Archaeology Magazine, would be the most resistant to the British precisely because they had been enslaved by them (Archaeology Magazine, 2024). Word spread, and the numbers grew: by 1738 more than 100 escapees had made their way to St. Augustine (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Florida Museum).

Not every arrival stayed free, and the founding turns on that failure. A group that included Francisco Menendez fought with the Yamasee against the English in the Yamasee War of 1715, then traveled south expecting the promised sanctuary, only to be claimed by the Yamasee leader Perro Bravo, purchased by the acting governor, and sold at auction in St. Augustine itself (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Landers biography). Menendez was bought by the royal accountant, Francisco Menendez Marquez, took his name at baptism, served in his household in a position of trust, and petitioned again and again for the freedom the edict promised (Archaeology Magazine, 2024). In 1726 he was appointed commander of the newly formed militia of formerly enslaved people, which helped repel an English attack in 1728, while still himself enslaved (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).

In 1738 the new governor, Manuel de Montiano, reviewed Menendez latest petition, which carried testimony from a Yamasee leader to his bravery against the English and blaming his continued enslavement on Perro Bravo. Montiano granted unconditional freedom to Menendez and the other re-enslaved escapees, and moved the militia to a new town of their own about two miles north of St. Augustine (Archaeology Magazine, 2024). The National Park Service women history page dates the grant to March 15, 1738: "all petitioners were granted freedom, and Fort Mose was established" (National Park Service). The town was named Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose, Royal Grace of Saint Teresa of Mose (National Park Service; National Historic Landmark nomination). Florida State Parks states the charter the same way: in 1738 the Spanish governor chartered the settlement as a settlement for those fleeing slavery from the English colonies in the Carolinas (Florida State Parks, History of Fort Mose).

The settlement stood on Mose Creek, a tributary of the Tolomato River flowing into Matanzas Bay, where trails heading north and west passed near the fort; the National Park Service notes the position guarded the northern approaches to St. Augustine, and that in exchange for the land the Black militia helped protect them (National Park Service, Fort Matanzas page). Distance is carried as about two miles; the same National Park Service page locates the present park about two and a half miles north of the historic district, a measurement of the modern park approach rather than the colonial townsite; see conflict 6.

Fort Mose was the first legally sanctioned free Black settlement in what is now the United States. Every authoritative source consulted states the distinction in those terms or in the National Park Service Fort Matanzas wording, "North America's first free legally sanctioned Black community" (National Park Service; Florida State Parks; Florida Museum; Florida Memory). No source consulted qualifies it away, and none gives the honor to any other town.

2. Building the Town

The first Fort Mose (1738-1740) was small, and the sources keep it small. Archaeology Magazine, from the documentary and archaeological record, describes a square fort of earth and logs about 70 feet on a side, surrounded by a moat, containing a lookout tower and a fortified house (Archaeology Magazine, 2024). The National Park Service describes an earthen-walled fort with thatched huts of Native type (the Park Service wording is "Indian-type thatched huts"), housing 38 men and their families, an estimated population of about 100 people (National Park Service, Fort Matanzas page). A second summary of the same record gives a four-sided fort, houses, and fields, with 38 households of men, women, and children, mostly runaways from the Carolinas (History of American Women blog, summarizing Deagan and MacMahon and Landers; carried here as a secondary summary, not as sole support). The community was ethnically African in many nations at once: the secondary literature lists Mandingos, Congos, Carabalis, Minas, and others among the residents, a diversity the parish and census records support in kind if not in that exact list (History of American Women summary; Landers, Black Society in Spanish Florida).

The residents adopted Spanish names and Spanish culture "with an African flavor," in the National Park Service phrase, became Catholics, and built a town, not only a fort: dwellings inside a walled area, a church, fields outside, and the fort itself (National Park Service; Wikipedia cross-check on the village plan). Florida State Parks adds the cultural point the curriculum should teach: the residents created a new community pulling from Native American, Spanish, and English customs as well as African ones (Florida State Parks, History of Fort Mose).

Daily life was farming, guard duty, and want, and the dossier does not dress it up. Spanish authorities envisioned Mose as an agricultural outpost that would grow enough corn, and possibly rice, to feed St. Augustine; the documents show it never produced enough to feed even itself and received meager government supplements that did not stave off hunger (Archaeology Magazine, 2024). The archaeological diet tells the other half: fish, shellfish, turtles, rabbits, and deer, a community living off the estuary and woods around it, with, in the newer excavations, some cow and pig bone suggesting limited livestock (Archaeology Magazine, 2024). The material record is spare: ceramics, gunflints and musket balls, cooking pots and corn-grinding stones, pipes and buttons, a rosary fragment and glass beads pointing to the Catholic life the bargain required, and, in Deagan summary, "almost no ornamental items or items of value... It was a life of privation" (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).

The second Fort Mose (1752-1763) was larger and better documented, because a priest described it. After twelve years in St. Augustine (1740-1752; see Section 6), the community was ordered back to Mose by Governor Garcia de Solis and built a second fort a few hundred yards north of the first (Archaeology Magazine, 2024). Father Juan Joseph de Solana 1759 report, as carried in Archaeology Magazine, describes at least seven internal structures including a church, a priest residence, and a guard house, inside earthen walls about 210 feet on a side on three sides, faced with clay and sod and covered with thorns, the east side open to Mose Creek, a moat about 10 feet wide, and small cannons and swivel guns on the bastions (Archaeology Magazine, 2024). The April 20, 1759 artillery inventory lists two iron cannons, four iron swivel guns, 24 muskets, and ammunition (Archaeology Magazine, 2024, caption to the inventory, Archivo General de Indias). Around the fort stood the village the 1759 census counts: 22 palm-thatch huts (Florida Museum historical archaeology page). A wharf on the creek side, identified in underwater excavations in the summer of 2023 by two lines of coquina stones and preserved wooden pilings, likely built by the Mose militia, explains the open east side: the fastest route to St. Augustine was by boat (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).

Government was the bargain made visible. Menendez was the town civil and military leader, addressed by Spanish authorities as they addressed Native chiefs, with the other militia members referred to as his subjects, and a resident priest ministered to the town as the Spanish ministered to Native mission villages (Landers biography; Archaeology Magazine, 2024). The militia pledge to the king, carried in the Landers biography, promised that the homesteaders would be "the most cruel enemies of the English" and would spill their "last drop of blood in defense of the Great Crown of Spain and the Holy Faith" (Landers biography, quoting the Mose declaration; Archaeology Magazine, 2024). That is the town politics in one sentence: freedom held by service, stated by the free men themselves as the price they had chosen.

3. Peak Years

Fort Mose had two lives, and each needs its own peak, stated in the numbers its own sources actually give.

The first life runs 1738 to 1740, only two years. Its size is an estimate, not a count: 38 men and their families, about 100 people (National Park Service); "more than 100 Africans" had arrived in St. Augustine by 1738 and "it is estimated that 100 Africans made Fort Mose their new home" (Florida Museum; Florida State Parks). That is the high-water figure for the first town, and it is an estimate in every source that carries it. The town peak in these years was positional as much as numerical: Mose was the northernmost defense of the Spanish capital, the first line of defense against attack from the British colonies, in the Florida Historical Society wording (Florida Historical Society, Florida Frontiers, Battle of Fort Mose).

The second life runs 1752 to 1763, eleven years, and its size is a count. In 1759 the village consisted of 22 palm-thatch huts housing 37 men, 15 women, 7 boys, and 8 girls: 67 people (Florida Museum historical archaeology page, from Landers archival recovery). The National Park Service women page counts 23 females in the same 1759 census; 15 adult women plus 8 girls is 23, so the two tellings agree arithmetically and this dossier notes the agreement rather than listing it as a conflict (National Park Service; Florida Museum). Most households were married couples and children (Legends of America census summary). The fort in these years was the larger second fort of Section 2, with its church, guard house, cannons, and wharf, and its people were, by the archaeological evidence, still poor and still living substantially off the land and water, with occasional access to traded goods as dear as an English patent medicine bottle that cost the rough equivalent of 23 present-day dollars (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).

At the evacuation in 1763 the colony consisted of 87 souls, in the abstract of Davidson, Lee, and Ibarrola return-to-Mose study (tDAR record 456794, 2020). That figure is larger than the 1759 census count of 67, which is consistent with four years of growth and with arrivals between the census and the evacuation, but no source consulted reconciles the two figures directly, and this dossier carries both with their dates attached; see conflict 3. Wider figures in the literature describe St. Augustine Black population as a whole, not Mose: about 3,000 Spanish subjects of all backgrounds evacuated in total (Landers, Floridanos in Cuba, 1996), and a Wikipedia-carried figure of some 400 enslaved and free Black inhabitants of St. Augustine and Mose together emigrating to Cuba. Neither is a Mose population, and neither is used as one here; see conflict 3.

The honest summary of the peak: Fort Mose was never large. At its documented largest it was a village of under 100 free people holding a frontier fort. Its significance was never size. It was that every one of those people was legally free, in a town of their own, a century and a quarter before emancipation in the United States, and that they held that freedom by arms for twenty-five years across two forts.

The People of Fort Mose

Francisco Menendez (dates uncertain; the Landers biography headnote gives c. 1709 to after 1763, with a 1759 census age of 55 suggesting birth about 1704; see conflict 8) is the central documented life. He was born in the Gambia region of West Africa, of Mandinga (Mandinka) people; his birth name is unknown. Captured and shipped across the Atlantic, he was enslaved in South Carolina, escaped in 1715 during the Yamasee War and fought alongside the Yamasee, was re-enslaved in Florida itself despite the sanctuary edict, took his name at baptism from his owner and godfather the royal accountant Francisco Menendez Marquez, learned to read and write Spanish (and, Landers notes, probably Arabic before it), commanded the free Black militia from 1726, won his unconditional freedom in 1738, and became captain of Fort Mose and its civil and military leader (Landers biography, African American National Biography; Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Enslaved.org biography page by Landers). He married Ana Maria de Escovar on December 28, 1739; they had four children (Landers biography; National Park Service women page). His later life is in Sections 5 and 6: the dawn retaking of Mose in 1740, the unanswered merits and services petitions to the king beginning December 12, 1740, capture at sea in July 1741 and a third enslavement ordered in the Bahamas, freedom again by 1743 by a route no source establishes, command of the second Mose from 1752, and evacuation to Cuba in 1763 with his wife and children (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Landers biography).

Ana Maria de Escovar was herself a Mandinga freedom seeker. The National Park Service women page tells her arc as far as the record carries it: enslaved in Carolina, she fled in 1715 with her husband (in that telling, before Menendez), was taken by Perro Bravo, reached St. Augustine, and was one of the 23 females counted in the 1759 census of Mose, where she and Francisco had four children and "continued to live at Fort Mose for some time" making "great contributions to St. Augustine" (National Park Service, women page). The parish registry for her 1739 marriage records both spouses as Mandingas who had once belonged to the royal accountant (Landers biography). The dossier carries both the Park Service telling and the registry fact and does not force them into one biography beyond what each source states.

Manuel de Montiano, governor of Spanish Florida, is the official who made the town legal: he reviewed Menendez petition, granted unconditional freedom to the re-enslaved petitioners, granted the land two miles north, and named the town (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; National Park Service). Antonio de Benavides, his predecessor, appointed Menendez to the militia command in 1726 (Archaeology Magazine, 2024). Fulgencio Garcia de Solis, governor from June 3, 1752 to April 18, 1755, ordered the 1752 return to Mose, in terms the record shows were coercive and contemptuous of the community culture; his own report is the source for the resistance and for his stated worry about the community "spiritual backwardness" and "bad customs" influencing St. Augustine (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Wikipedia entry for Garcia de Solis, cross-check only).

The community itself is a person in this dossier, because the records name it better than they name most of its members. The 1759 census household the Florida Museum education packet uses as its teaching example is Menendez own household (Florida Museum, Fort Mose Education Packet, 2023 edition of the 1991 guide). Beyond the captain household, the records preserve a town of married couples and children in most households (Legends of America summary of the census; Florida Museum), women who, in Florida Memory general account of free Black Florida, contributed to the economy, owned land, and used the Spanish legal system, and men who stood guard, patrolled the frontier, farmed, fished, and worked the water (Florida Memory; Florida Museum). The militia members who resisted the 1752 relocation, unnamed in Governor Garcia report, are also part of the people record: families who "had married in town, taken up trades, and had children" in St. Augustine and did not want to leave a more comfortable life for the frontier post again, in Deagan summary of the report (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).

The keepers of the record belong here too. Kathleen Deagan, archaeologist of the Florida Museum of Natural History, led the 1986-1988 excavations that identified the second fort site and has called the Mose excavation her most important work (Florida Historical Society, Florida Frontiers, March 29, 2016; Archaeology Magazine, 2024). Jane Landers, historian at Vanderbilt University, recovered the Mose families names and lives from the colonial archives of Spain, Florida, Cuba, and South Carolina, wrote Black Society in Spanish Florida (1999) and Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose (1992), and co-led the documentary side of the Fort Mose project (Florida Museum; Archaeology Magazine, 2024). John Marron of the University of Florida was the third named lead of the 1986-1988 research team (Wikipedia cross-check). The current excavation generation, Lori Lee of Flagler College, Liz Ibarrola of the University of Texas at Austin, James Davidson of the University of Florida, and Chuck Meide of the Lighthouse Archaeological Maritime Program, has worked the site from 2019 forward (Archaeology Magazine, 2024). Charles Ellis, president of the Fort Mose Historical Society and one of its 1996 founders, cut the ribbon on the reconstructed fort on May 9, 2025 (St. Johns County, May 13, 2025).

4. The Turning Point

Trauma-informed framing for this section and for any lesson built from it: the facts below are stated in plain sequence without graphic detail. Students should encounter Mose first as the living free town of Sections 1 to 4, and the battle second, as the documented defense of that town. Casualty figures are carried as the conflicting reports they are, not as a single settled toll.

The turning point is the Battle of Bloody Mose, June 1740, inside the War of Jenkins Ear between Britain and Spain (1739-1748). The sequence on which the authoritative sources agree:

  • September 1739: the Stono Rebellion in South Carolina, one of the largest rebellions by enslaved people in colonial North America, is crushed; English colonists blame the Spanish promise of freedom at St. Augustine in part for inspiring it, and war between Britain and Spain follows (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).
  • May 1740: General James Oglethorpe, founder and governor of Georgia, invades Spanish Florida with a combined force of British regulars, Highland (Scottish) troops, South Carolina and Georgia militia and volunteers, and Native allies, and besieges St. Augustine (History of American Women summary; Florida Memory, The British Invasion).
  • Before the attack: Governor Montiano orders the people of Mose to abandon their settlement and fall back to St. Augustine for safety; some Mose defenders are killed by Native allies of the British during the approach, in the Black Catholic Messenger summary of the record (National Park Service, Castillo page; Black Catholic Messenger, 2023). The evacuated families shelter in the city and at the Castillo de San Marcos.
  • The occupation: British forces capture and occupy the empty Fort Mose and use it as a base against St. Augustine; Florida Memory specifies a company of Scottish Highlanders and a number of Creeks inside the fort, under Colonel Palmer, who warned his men to be on alert the evening before the attack (Florida Memory, The British Invasion; National Park Service).
  • The retaking, at dawn: a combined force of Spanish regulars, the Mose militia under Captain Francisco Menendez, and Native (in Florida Historical Society reenactment description, Yamasee) auxiliaries surprises the occupiers and retakes the fort (Florida Memory; Florida Historical Society). Florida Memory dates the attack to the early morning of June 15 by the contemporary calendar, modern calendar June 26; most modern summaries give June 26, 1740 outright; June 14 appears in two secondary tellings. The date division is recorded as conflict 2 and the dossier headline date is June 26, 1740 (New Style), with the Old Style equivalent stated.
  • The outcome: the British force inside Mose is routed; the siege of St. Augustine loses its northern position and, after Cuban reinforcements reach the city in the standard accounts, Oglethorpe retreats to Georgia (History of American Women summary; Florida Historical Society). Governor Montiano commends Menendez for valor and leadership, and Menendez own December 12, 1740 petition asks the king for recognition and recompense for the fighting (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Black Catholic Messenger summary). The price is the town itself: the fort is destroyed in the battle and Mose ceases to exist as a place for twelve years (National Park Service; Archaeology Magazine, 2024).

Casualties, carried as reports, not as fact: Florida Memory states plainly that conflicting reports surfaced on English casualties, carrying Oglethorpe report of 20 Highlanders killed (with the sentence truncated in the capture consulted) against other counts (Florida Memory, The British Invasion). A podcast transcript account gives more than half of about 170 British occupiers and allies killed or captured, against 10 killed and 20 wounded on the Spanish side (Stuff You Missed in History Class transcript, carried here as a secondary account only). The Florida Historical Society reenactment account quotes a figure of 137 British troops at Mose of whom 25 got out unharmed, uncaptured, or alive (Florida Historical Society). These figures do not agree, no authoritative source consulted settles them, and the dossier adopts none of them; see conflict 9. The British name for the fight, Bloody Mose (in some contemporary British spelling, Bloody Moosa), is itself the period evidence that the retaking was costly to the occupiers (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Siege of Fort Mose summary).

What the turning point turned: Mose proved, in the first test, that its militia would fight for the freedom Spain had granted, and it lost its home by winning. The community that had built a town in 1738 spent the next twelve years as residents of St. Augustine, and the man who led the retaking spent part of that interval petitioning an unanswering king, sailing as a corsair, and surviving a third enslavement (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Landers biography). The battle is therefore taught in this curriculum as both a victory and a displacement, because the record holds both.

5. Survival, Decline, or Destruction

The twelve years between the forts (1740-1752) were not an absence; they were a second founding inside the city. The people of Mose lived within St. Augustine, where, in Deagan summary of the later relocation report, many had married in town, taken up trades, and had children, in a larger and more comfortable social community than the frontier post had offered (Archaeology Magazine, 2024). Menendez served as a Spanish corsair, was captured in July 1741, was ordered sold in the Bahamas, and was free in St. Augustine again by 1743 (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).

In 1752 Governor Fulgencio Garcia de Solis decided, and then insisted, that the Black militia return to Mose. The National Park Service states the move plainly: the governor "insisted they return and rebuild the settlement in 1752" (National Park Service, Fort Matanzas page). Archaeology Magazine, from Garcia own report, adds what the insistence met: substantial resistance from militiamen who expressed their "desire to live in complete liberty," a light punishment of unnamed leaders of the resistance, and relocation of the militia with their wives and children to the new site a few hundred yards north of the original fort (Archaeology Magazine, 2024). The rebuilding was therefore coerced, and this dossier says so rather than presenting the second Mose as a simple homecoming. The community rebuilt anyway, larger than before: the second fort and village of Sections 2 and 4, again under Menendez leadership, stood from 1752 to 1763 (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; National Park Service).

The end came by treaty, not by attack. In 1763 the Treaty of Paris ended the French and Indian War and transferred Florida from Spain to Britain (National Park Service; Florida Memory). For the people of Mose, British rule meant the power that had enslaved them, and the community chose exile over the risk of re-enslavement: they evacuated to Cuba with the rest of the Spanish population of St. Augustine (National Park Service; Archaeology Magazine, 2024). Davidson, Lee, and Ibarrola count the evacuated colony at 87 souls (tDAR record 456794). The wider evacuation carried about 3,000 Spanish subjects in total, Spanish, African, and Native, to Cuba (Landers, Floridanos in Cuba, 1996).

In Cuba the Mose people kept their freedom and, for a time, their town. After a few years in Regla, a fishing village near Havana, the Florida evacuees were granted land in Matanzas Province, where in 1764 the landowner Simon Rodriguez donated land and 84 Florida families in the wider account relocated to the new settlement of San Agustin de la Nueva Florida, at Ceiba Mocha (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Aponte study summary of Landers, 1996). Each household there was given tools, a donkey, land, seeds, and money in the Florida Star account of the University of Florida research, and, in a documented irony the dossier records without emphasis, an enslaved person to help clear the land (Florida Star, November 7, 1992, via University of Florida Digital Collections; Archaeology Magazine, 2024). The ground was rocky and poor, the settlement was isolated and lived by subsistence agriculture, and within a decade or so most Mose families had moved back to Regla; Menendez, his wife Ana Maria, and their four children likely among them, which is the last the record knows of him (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Landers biography). Landers Cuba research follows the community further: Mose families sold their San Agustin de la Nueva Florida properties to Canary Island families, and modern residents of Ceiba Mocha, in Landers finding, have no knowledge of the town multiracial founding and believe it was settled exclusively by Canary Islanders; the Ceiba Mocha church, Nuestra Senora de la Candelaria, built in 1793 in the Florida Star account, holds images of St. Augustine alongside the Canary Islands patron (Landers, 1996; Florida Star, 1992). No direct descendants of Fort Mose have been identified at Ceiba Mocha (Florida Star, 1992). The survival, then, is documentary and memorial rather than continuous: the Mose community survived as free people in Cuba, and its Florida town survived in archives in Spain, Florida, Cuba, and South Carolina until Landers and Deagan work recovered it (Archaeology Magazine, 2024; Florida Historical Society).

The fort site after 1763 had its own decline. The British refurbished the fort and used it during their occupation to 1783; Spain used it again as an outpost in the Second Spanish period; and in 1812, during the Patriot War, a Spanish schooner cannonaded and burned it to dislodge American occupiers, its final destruction (Archaeology Magazine, 2024). The ruins of the second fort were still visible in the 1850s; in the 1880s Flagler dredging turned the Mose ground into salt marsh and islands, and the site disappeared from view until archaeology returned (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).

6. The Town Today

Nothing of the original town stands above ground, and the dossier says so plainly before it says what does stand. The original earth and wooden structures left no remains (Florida State Parks). What a visitor finds today is at Fort Mose Historic State Park, 15 Fort Mose Trail, St. Augustine, FL 32084, in St. Johns County (Florida State Parks; St. Johns County).

The park: a 40-acre waterfront historic site administered by the Florida Park Service (through Anastasia State Park in the older National Park Service account), preserving the archaeological site of the second fort (1752-1763) on its marsh island (Florida State Parks; National Park Service). Acreage is carried as the park present 40 acres; the National Park Service older page gives a 24-acre acquired site, and a visiting account gives 41 acres purchased in 1989 with additions in 1998 and 2004; the three figures describe different acquisition stages and are not reconciled in this draft; see the Research log (National Park Service; VISIT FLORIDA). On the mainland the park offers a visitor center and interactive museum telling the full story of Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose, with artifacts from the excavations, a film, the self-guided Flight to Freedom Trail with interpretive panels beginning at the year 1738, an observation boardwalk, picnic areas, birding, and a kayak launch (Florida State Parks). Grounds are open daily 9 a.m. to 5 p.m.; the visitor center and museum are open 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. Thursday through Monday, closed Tuesday and Wednesday, with a 2 dollar museum fee per adult and fort tours at 10 a.m. and 1 p.m. on open days (Florida State Parks, Hours and Fees). Hours and fees change; verify with the park before any curriculum visit is published.

Since May 9, 2025, the park also holds a full-scale, life-sized reconstruction of the 1738 fort, more than 39 feet tall with a historically accurate moat and landscaping and period furnishings, built a short distance from the original site at a final cost of 3.2 million dollars, funded from public and private sources including a 2022 Florida African American Cultural and Historical Grants Program award and a 250,000 dollar St. Johns County contribution, and designed with the guidance of Deagan and Landers (St. Johns County, May 13, 2025; Florida State Parks Foundation release, May 14, 2025; Florida State Parks, History of Fort Mose: "the reconstruction of the historic 1738 fort is complete"). The reconstruction is a representation, not the original fort, and teaching materials must label it so.

Designations and keeping organizations:

  • National Historic Landmark and National Register of Historic Places, both October 12, 1994, reference number 94001645 (National Register record; Florida State Parks).
  • Premier site on the Florida Black Heritage Trail (National Park Service; Florida Museum).
  • Precursor site, National Underground Railroad Network to Freedom, named by the National Park Service in 2009 (Florida Historic Coast, 2023).
  • Site of Memory, UNESCO Slave Route Project (Florida Museum; the 2019 listing year is carried on the county history account in Section 10).
  • The Fort Mose Historical Society, founded 1996, the citizen organization that hosts reenactments including the annual Battle of Bloody Mose reenactment (held each June; the Society signature event), militia musters, and the Discover Fort Mose Jazz and Blues Series each February, and that pursued the fort reconstruction as a goal from the mid-1990s (Fort Mose Historical Society; Florida Historic Coast; Wikipedia cross-check).
  • The Florida Museum of Natural History, holder of the excavation collections and producer of the Fort Mose online exhibit and education packet (Florida Museum).
  • Active archaeology: the 2019-2024 investigations by Flagler College, the University of Florida, the University of Texas at Austin, and the Lighthouse Archaeological Maritime Program continue the Deagan and Landers work, under a stated threat: the marsh island site is being lost to rising sea levels, with little likely to remain above water in a decade or two on the Archaeology Magazine assessment (Archaeology Magazine, 2024).

The curriculum should prepare students for the shape of the visit: a mainland museum and trail, a marsh island they view rather than walk, and, since 2025, a reconstructed fort that lets them stand inside the scale of the 1738 original without being the original. The absence of the town itself, like its recovery from archives and marsh, is part of the record.

Conflicts between sources, recorded not smoothed

1. Founding year: 1738 against 1739. The National Park Service, Florida State Parks, the Florida Museum, Archaeology Magazine, and the project record all give 1738, with a March 15, 1738 freedom grant in the National Park Service women account. Florida Memory, in two separate exhibits, states that free Black residents established Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose in 1739, and the National Park Service corridor account says fugitive slaves inhabited Fort Mose as early as 1739. The likely reconciliation, grant and charter in 1738 with occupation and building running into 1739, is stated by no source consulted in those words and is not assumed here. The dossier carries 1738 as the founding year, on the weight of the sources, and records 1739 here. 2. The Bloody Mose date in June 1740. Florida Memory dates the dawn attack to June 15 contemporary calendar, modern calendar June 26. Most modern summaries give June 26, 1740 outright (Florida Historical Society; Wikipedia cross-check; podcast transcript account). Two secondary tellings give June 14 (History of American Women summary; Black Catholic Messenger). June 14 and June 15 differ by a day within Old Style reporting, and June 26 is the New Style equivalent of June 15. The dossier headlines June 26, 1740 (New Style) and records all three. 3. Population figures. About 100 at the first settlement is an estimate in every carrier (National Park Service; Florida State Parks; Florida Museum). The 1759 census count is exact on its own terms: 67 (Florida Museum). The 1763 evacuation count of 87 souls (Davidson et al., 2020) exceeds the 1759 count and is not reconciled with it by any source consulted. Wider figures are not Mose figures: some 400 enslaved and free Black inhabitants of St. Augustine and Mose together emigrating to Cuba (Wikipedia-carried figure), a roughly 300-member Cuba successor community (Florida Star, 1992), and about 3,000 total evacuees of all backgrounds (Landers, 1996). The Key Facts Box marks peak population NOT ESTABLISHED and Sections 4 and 6 carry each number with its scope. 4. National Historic Landmark year: 1994 against 1995. The National Register record, Florida State Parks, the National Park Service, and St. Johns County all give October 12, 1994. The Florida Museum historical archaeology page states Mose was named a National Historic Landmark in 1995, and one Menendez biography mirror repeats 1995. The dossier carries 1994 and records 1995 as the divergent museum and biography wording. 5. The terms of freedom. Catholic conversion is constant. Militia service is stated as a general condition by Florida State Parks ("allegiance to the king and membership in the Catholic Church" are its only stipulations in one passage) and by Florida Memory (military service and Catholic acceptance), while the Siege of Fort Mose summary and several popular accounts specify that men who passed military inspection earned freedom after four years of militia service under the 1693 edict. Montiano 1738 grant is separately described as unconditional by Archaeology Magazine and Landers. The dossier carries conversion and service as the lived bargain and the four-year term as a variant attached to the edict, and does not merge them. 6. Distance from St. Augustine. The dossier follows the detailed record and records the Park Service compression here. 8. Francisco Menendez birth year.
