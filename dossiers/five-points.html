Key Facts Box

  • Founded: c. 1820, carried as a closest estimate. The anchors are two institutions planted in that year: the African Society for Mutual Relief moved into its hall at 42 Orange Street, bought by Juliette Toussaint for $1,800, and African Free School No. 2 opened on Mulberry Street (Columbia University, Mapping the African American Past; BlackPast, "The New York African Society for Mutual Relief"; New York Public Library, #BlackNYers).
  • Founded by: no single founder in the town sense. Five Points was not founded by a proprietor, a railroad, or a land company. The neighborhood as a Black community was built in effect by its residents and institutions: the free Black families who settled on the filled Collect Pond ground in the early 1800s, and the members of the New York African Society for Mutual Relief who bought their hall at 42 Orange Street in 1820.
  • Incorporated: never. Five Points was a neighborhood inside New York City, governed as part of the city and principally identified with its Sixth Ward; it had no municipal incorporation, charter, or town government of its own.
  • Peak population: not established as a neighborhood total. No source publishes a defensible all-time peak population for the Five Points neighborhood as bounded; the documented figures are citywide and block-level counts, not a neighborhood peak.
  • Population today: none as Five Points. The neighborhood no longer exists as a residential community; its ground is now occupied by the Civic Center around Foley Square and by Chinatown.
  • Known for: one of New York City's first free Black settlements, raised over the drained and filled Collect Pond; the African Society for Mutual Relief hall, African Free School No. 2, St. Philip's Episcopal Church, and Mother AME Zion's first services on Cross Street; Black, Irish, and German neighbors living together early and at scale; and Almack's dance hall at 67 Orange Street, where William Henry Lane, called Master Juba, danced for Charles Dickens in 1842 and where Black and Irish dance forms met in what became tap dance.
  • The song: "Five Points, New York," this town's song in the All Black Towns series.

Introduction

  • Town name, State: Five Points, New York
  • County: New York County (Manhattan), New York City. The neighborhood lay in the city's Sixth Ward for most of its documented life.
  • Status: historic neighborhood, vanished as a residential district. The former area is now occupied by the Civic Center around Foley Square and by Chinatown. Five Points is a neighborhood, not a separate town: it was never incorporated and has never had a municipal government of its own.
  • Dossier version: draft v0.1, 2026-11-09

Five Points was one of New York City's first free Black settlements, raised in Lower Manhattan over the drained Collect Pond. The name comes from the five-cornered intersection formed when Anthony Street was extended east to the junction of Cross and Orange Streets; the streets are now Worth Street, Baxter Street, and Mosco Street in its surviving remnant.

The neighborhood as a Black community was built in effect by its residents and institutions: the free Black families who settled on the filled pond ground in the early 1800s, the African Society for Mutual Relief, whose hall at 42 Orange Street was bought in 1820, African Free School No. 2 on Mulberry Street, St. Philip's Episcopal Church, and the congregation that became Mother AME Zion Church, which first worshipped on Cross Street in 1796.

Five Points became an early large-scale instance of Black, Irish, and German neighbors living together, and a place the wider city knew through sensational journalism as a slum, a label its archaeology and its Black institutional record contradict in part. The chapters that follow take the story in order, from the founding to the ground today.

Timeline: the story at a glance

1644the first group of Africans in New Amsterdam petitions for and receives half-freedom, with land grants north of the town; about thirty Black-owned grants follow, some 300 acres from the Bowery Road near Collect Pond toward 34th Street (New York Public Library, #BlackNYers).
1690s to 1790sover 15,000 free and enslaved Africans are buried in the African Burial Ground in Lower Manhattan, just south of Collect Pond, after Africans are barred in 1697 from the city's primary burial ground (National Park Service, African Burial Ground National Monument).
About 1809Anthony Street is extended east to the junction of Cross and Orange Streets, creating the five-cornered intersection that gives the neighborhood its name (Wikipedia, "Five Points, Manhattan," citing Anbinder).
1811the landfill of Collect Pond is completed, and homes are soon built on the reclaimed land; the poorly engineered fill subsides, and most middle and upper class inhabitants leave (Wikipedia, "Collect Pond"; "Five Points, Manhattan").
1820Juliette Toussaint buys a tenement and rear meeting hall at 42 Orange Street for the African Society for Mutual Relief, and African Free School No. 2 opens at 135-137 Mulberry Street, a brick building for 500 students (BlackPast; New York Public Library, #BlackNYers).
July 4, 1827slavery ends in New York State for people born before July 4, 1799; in the same year Samuel Cornish and John Russwurm found Freedom's Journal, the first African American owned and operated newspaper in the United States, and George Catlin paints the Five Points intersection (New York Public Library).
July 1834anti-abolition riots run for a series of nights; in Five Points, white mobs loot St. Philip's Episcopal Church and the African Society for Mutual Relief hall, and Black residents flee (Wikipedia, "Five Points, Manhattan"; FABnyc).
1837Dr. James McCune Smith, an African Free School graduate who earned his medical degree at the University of Glasgow after American schools denied him admission, returns to New York and opens a pharmacy at 93 West Broadway (Columbia University, Mapping the African American Past).
1842Charles Dickens tours Five Points and writes about it in American Notes; at Almack's dance hall, 67 Orange Street, he watches the dancer the dance histories identify as William Henry Lane, Master Juba (BlackPast; Bedford and Bowery).
1854Anthony, Orange, and Cross Streets are renamed Worth, Baxter, and Park Streets (Wikipedia, "Five Points, Manhattan").
