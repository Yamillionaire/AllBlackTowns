ANCHOR FLAG, stated plainly: 1764 marks the town's origin as a French trading post. It does NOT mark a Black founding. The catalog community's documented presence is post-Civil War Black landowners, on the project's basis.
  • Founded by: no single founder is established in the sources consulted. The 1764 act was institutional, not personal: France established the Opelousas Post slightly north of the contemporary city of Opelousas, near present-day Washington. The Black community of the town and parish has no single founder in the record either: it is documented as a population, free people of color in the parish before the Civil War and Black residents, landowners, students, and church members in and around the town across the nineteenth century, rather than as one founding person or family.
  • Incorporated: 1835, under the current name Washington (Wikipedia, "Washington, Louisiana").
  • Peak population: 1,528 in the 1910 U.S. Census, the highest figure in the decennial series consulted, which runs from 536 in 1860 to 742 in 2020 (U.S. Decennial Census figures as tabulated in Wikipedia, "Washington, Louisiana").
  • Population today: 742 in the 2020 U.S. Census, with 382 households and 212 families, and a racial composition of 410 Black or African American residents (55.26 percent, non-Hispanic) (U.S. Census Bureau figures as tabulated in Wikipedia, "Washington, Louisiana").
  • Known for: the largest inland port between New Orleans and St. Louis for much of the nineteenth century; the head of steam packet navigation up Bayou Courtableau, with 91 steam packets in the Courtableau trade in 1860 (National Register nomination); the Grimble Bell School, the earliest African American school in St. Landry Parish, opened in the 1830s for the children of free people of color and forced closed by white vigilantes in 1860; the September 13, 1868 meeting in Washington, one of the documented steps into the Opelousas Massacre; and a historic district listed in the National Register on November 15, 1978 that covers about 80 percent of the town and about 260 nineteenth and early twentieth century structures.
  • The song: "Washington, Louisiana." Its full lyrics are printed in The Song chapter of this dossier, transcribed from the finished master recording and checked line by line against it.

Introduction

  • Town name, State: Washington, Louisiana
  • Parish: St. Landry Parish
  • Status: still incorporated and inhabited. The town remains governed, with a mayor and council, and it is not a ghost town.
  • Dossier version: draft v0.1, 2026-11-09

Washington is a real, still inhabited town in St. Landry Parish, Louisiana, on Bayou Courtableau, a few miles north of Opelousas, the parish seat. It was not founded as an all-Black town. Its place in this collection rests on a different, documented record: a very old river town, founded as a colonial trading post, in a parish that held one of the largest free populations of color in rural Louisiana before the Civil War, with the parish's earliest African American school inside the town, with a Black majority in the modern census counts, and with post-Civil War Black landownership and commerce carried in the project's record. The founding year carried for this town is 1764, as a closest estimate and a town-origin anchor: it marks the town's beginning as the Post of Opelousas, a French trading post, later called Church Landing. It does not mark a Black founding.

A note on care: Washington's record includes enslavement in its parish from the 1740s, the forced closing of a Black school by vigilantes in 1860, wartime seizure of supplies, and a central Washington role in the events that led into the Opelousas Massacre of September 28, 1868, one of the bloodiest massacres of the Reconstruction era. This dossier follows the curriculum trauma-informed rule throughout: documented fact first, restrained language, no spectacle.

1. Founding

Washington was not founded once. It was founded in layers: a colonial trading post, a church landing, a steamboat port town, and only in 1835 a municipality under the name Washington. This chapter keeps the layers separate, because the record does.

The deepest layer in the sources consulted is human presence and trade before any post. The parish history records that Jean Joseph LeKintrek and Joseph Blainpain, in partnership to trade with the Opelousa Indians, came into the Opelousas country in the early 1740s and brought three enslaved Africans, the first to live in the area on that account (Wikipedia, "St. Landry Parish, Louisiana"). Two things must be said plainly about that entry. First, it is parish history, not town history: it places enslaved Africans in the Opelousas country a generation before the 1764 post, and it does not place them in a town that did not yet exist in that form. Second, it establishes the moral ground of the whole dossier: from the first dated record consulted, Black presence in this country is present as enslaved presence, brought by traders. The free Black community that later fills this record, the planters, the school, the church, grew in the same country against that beginning. Neither fact cancels the other, and the curriculum teaches both.

The post itself is dated 1764 in the records the project relies on. Washington was founded as the Post of Opelousas in 1764 as a French trading post (Wikipedia, "Washington, Louisiana"). The parish history states the same institutional act from the other side: in 1764 France established the Opelousas Post slightly north of the contemporary city of Opelousas, near present-day Washington (Wikipedia, "St. Landry Parish, Louisiana"). The project carries 1764 as a closest estimate, on exactly this basis, and flags the number everywhere it appears: 1764 is a town-origin anchor. It does not mark a Black founding.

Between the post and the town sits the church. Priests traveled by boat to the community to hold Mass beginning in the 1750s, coming and going for years without a church building, until the first church stood at the landing, named in Spanish for the Immaculate Conception of the Post of Opelousas. A church at the landing is what gave the place its second name, Church Landing, and the parish that became Immaculate Conception began here before it was later moved to Opelousas. The name sequence, on the combined record, is therefore: Post of Opelousas (1764, carried), Church Landing (the name tied to the first church, dated as a name to 1822 in the Wikipedia town history), and Washington (incorporation, 1835). Wikipedia states the sequence in one sentence: founded as the Post of Opelousas in 1764, the post later became known as Church Landing in 1822, and in 1835 the town was incorporated under the current name (Wikipedia, "Washington, Louisiana").

Land, in the founding sense, Washington never had a Black founding purchase to record, and this dossier says so rather than reaching for one. There is no townsite purchase by Black founders, no homestead colony, no allotment, and no Exoduster migration in any source consulted.

The land story that is documented runs the other way, and it belongs in this chapter because it is the ground the Black community stood on: fertile parish land well suited to agriculture and cattle raising, a bayou route to New Orleans cleared by subscription in the 1790s, and plantations whose produce made the port (National Register nomination). Inside that plantation country, free people of color in St. Landry Parish held land and wealth on a scale the record singles out: outside New Orleans, St. Landry and Natchitoches Parishes had the largest numbers of free people of color, and French-speaking free people of color lived in rural areas especially in St. Landry, Natchitoches, Pointe Coupee, and St. Martin Parishes (64 Parishes, "Free People of Color"). The founding chapter therefore ends where the evidence ends: the post is 1764 on the carried estimate, the Black community's presence in the parish is documented from the colonial period forward, and a Washington-specific Black founding act is not established.
