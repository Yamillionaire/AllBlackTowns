Key Facts Box

  • Founded: 1877. The Nicodemus Town Company was organized in April 1877; the first settler is reported to have arrived June 18, 1877, and the main recruited party from Kentucky arrived in September 1877 (NPS park brochure; BlackPast; town histories summarized in Section 9).
  • Founded by: the Nicodemus Town Company. The members named most often are the Reverend W. H. Smith, a Black minister (company president), and W. R. Hill, a white land developer (company treasurer), together with five other Black men from Lexington, Kentucky; the named six Black founders in one standard account are W. H. Smith, Benjamin Carr, Jerry Allsap, the Reverend Simon P. Roundtree, Jeff Lenze, and William Edmonds (NPS park brochure; BlackPast). The exact membership count is disputed; see conflict 1 in Section 9.
  • Incorporated: not applicable. Nicodemus was never incorporated; it has always been an unincorporated townsite governed through Nicodemus Township (Wikipedia community summary; Graham County township structure).
  • Peak population: NOT ESTABLISHED as a single agreed figure. Reported figures conflict by geography and source: about 600 residents in the town and farming community in the first decade of the 1900s (Contemporary Black Biography encyclopedia entry); 595 in the federal census figure reported for the area in 1910 (University of Kansas Libraries collection summary); about 700 in the mid-1880s (NPS-derived family guide); nearly 1,000 at the high end (Trust for Public Land). The 1880 counts alone disagree (see conflict 5). Do not publish one number until the townsite-versus-township basis is settled.
  • Population today: 14 (Nicodemus CDP, 2020 federal census, via Wikipedia's census citation). Nicodemus Township, which includes the surrounding farm families, counted 43 in the 2020 census (Wikipedia township summary). The project's outreach plan carries "about 14 to 25 residents," consistent with the CDP figure plus nearby households.
  • Known for: the oldest and only remaining Black town west of the Mississippi River founded by formerly enslaved settlers in the Reconstruction era; an Exoduster colony that predates and helped inspire the 1879 Exoduster migration; the Emancipation and Homecoming Celebration held since 1878; a National Historic Site preserving five historic buildings.
  • The song: "Nicodemus, Kansas" (see Section 7).

1. Founding

Nicodemus was a planned colony, not an accident of migration. As Reconstruction collapsed in the South, a group of Black men from Lexington, Kentucky, together with a white land promoter, organized the Nicodemus Town Company in April 1877 to found an all-Black town on the Kansas plains, where Black settlers could own land, vote, and govern themselves (NPS; BlackPast). W. H. Smith, a Black minister who had already homesteaded in the area, served as company president; W. R. Hill, a white land developer from Indiana, served as treasurer (town histories via Legends of America; Kansas City Public Library). The company platted a townsite in the Solomon Valley of Graham County, in northwestern Kansas, and recruited settlers in Kentucky and Tennessee with circulars and church visits; the Reverend Simon P. Roundtree recruited in Kentucky, and Benjamin "Pap" Singleton, the Nashville organizer later called the Moses of the Colored Exodus, also distributed the company's circulars (Legends of America, summarizing the standard histories).

The name carried the founding argument. The founders named the town for Nicodemus, remembered in Black tradition as a legendary African slave prince who purchased his freedom (BlackPast; Encyclopedia.com), and Angela Bates, the town historian and a descendant, adds the biblical Nicodemus and the plantation song line "Wake Nicodemus" for the day of Jubilee as the name's other roots (Kansas City Public Library). Both derivations circulate; see conflict 2.

Settlement came in stages, and the sources do not agree on the numbers (conflict 3). Roundtree is reported as the first settler, arriving June 18, 1877 (town histories). Zachary T. Fletcher and his wife Jenny Smith Fletcher, the Reverend Smith's daughter, arrived in July 1877; Fletcher became company secretary, then the town's first postmaster and its first entrepreneur (BlackPast; town histories). One encyclopedia account counts the first thirty colonists in July 1877 and 150 more in March 1878 (Encyclopedia.com). The National Park Service brochure gives the main arrival as some 300 settlers recruited from Kentucky in September 1877, and records that sixty of them, shaken by the dugout dwellings, returned to Kentucky immediately (NPS park brochure).

What the September party found was a plat on paper and a prairie in fact: no houses, no trees, no shade, only tall grass (NPS-derived family guide). Families wintered in dugouts, pits dug into the earth and roofed over, along the Solomon River draws. Williana Hickman, who arrived with her husband the Reverend Daniel Hickman in the spring of 1878, remembered being told the smoke rising from the ground was Nicodemus, and crying at the sight (her later account, quoted in town histories). Settlers took up homestead claims of 160 acres around the townsite under the Homestead Act of 1862 while the company sold town lots; the legal title to the townsite itself was later challenged by land speculator Henry Miller, and the town received its official title on June 6, 1886 (Wikipedia history summary; flagged in Section 9 as needing a primary-source check).

The first winter nearly broke the colony. Through the winter of 1878-79 the settlers depended in part on relief supplies solicited by agents such as the Reverend Silas M. Lee, shipped free by the Kansas Pacific railroad and issued from a commissary by town officers; in April 1879 the Nicodemus Town Company was abolished and each man left to, in the period phrase, hoe his own row (Kansas Historical Society digital collections, historical article on the colony). The town survived the aid winter, and its timing mattered: Nicodemus was founded in 1877, two years before the great Exoduster migration of 1879, and its circulars and example helped draw that later wave toward Kansas (Kansas Historical Society, Kansapedia; Kansas City Public Library).

2. Building the Town

Government came early and ran through county and township offices, because Nicodemus was never incorporated. Graham County was organized on April 1, 1880, when Governor John P. St. John proclaimed its organization and appointed Nicodemus resident Edwin P. McCabe temporary county clerk (Erbschloe chronology, drawn from the NPS historic resource study). In the June 1, 1880 county election, John DePrad of Nicodemus was elected county clerk, and other Black residents won township offices including justice of the peace, township clerk, and road overseer (Erbschloe chronology). In 1880 Nicodemus also lost the vote for county seat to Millbrook (Wikipedia history summary). McCabe, who had arrived in 1878 and worked as a land agent and attorney, was elected county clerk in November 1881 and then, in 1882, was elected state auditor of Kansas, at the time the highest elective office held by a Black man outside the South (BlackPast; Encyclopedia.com).

Institutions, in a dated spine:

  • 1877: the first worship in dugouts; the First Baptist congregation traces its worship to a crude dugout in 1877, then a sod church, before its limestone building (HistoryNet, drawing on NPS site interpretation).
  • August 1, 1878: the first Emancipation Day celebration in Nicodemus, marking August 1, 1834, the day emancipation took effect in the British West Indies; the celebration has continued annually since, later renamed Homecoming and now held the last weekend of July (Christian Recorder / CSJ Kansas account of Angela Bates's telling; Contemporary Black Biography entry; see conflict 7 on the founding year).
  • 1878: post office established, with Zachary Fletcher as first postmaster; the post office, the first school, and Fletcher's St. Francis Hotel all operated from the Fletcher building (BlackPast; community history document). Jenny Smith Fletcher was the town's first schoolteacher (BlackPast).
  • 1878: the African Methodist Episcopal congregation begins worship; it worshipped at another site until acquiring its present building in 1897 (HistoryNet; see conflict 8).
  • 1879: first retail stores open; S. G. Wilson erects the general store, the town's first two-story building (BlackPast; Encyclopedia.com).
  • By 1881: about 35 structures stand in town, including three hotels, two livery stables, a blacksmith shop, a lumber yard, two churches, and two dry goods stores (Erbschloe chronology).
  • May 13, 1886: the Western Cyclone, the town's first newspaper, publishes its first issue under editor Arthur G. Tallman (Annals of Kansas, 1886; Erbschloe chronology). A second paper, the Nicodemus Enterprise, follows in 1887 (BlackPast; Encyclopedia.com).
  • 1887: the Bank of Nicodemus opens under A. L. McPherson (Erbschloe chronology). A freestanding School District No. 1 schoolhouse is erected in 1887, with two teachers employed for a nine-month term (Erbschloe chronology; HistoryNet).

The economy was farming first and town commerce second. The 1881 township statistics in the NPS-drawn chronology count an average of 12 acres per homestead in cultivation, with corn, millet, and sorghum as the listed crops, and livestock in modest numbers (Erbschloe chronology). R. B. Scruggs, an early colonist who freighted wagons to Stockton and worked railroad towns across western Kansas, built his holding to 720 acres over time, an example the colony's historians used of how Nicodemus men combined wage work off the claim with proving up their own land (Kansas Historical Society digital collections).

3. Peak Years

The peak runs from about 1886 through the first decade of the 1900s (University of Kansas Libraries: the town reached its most prosperous years by 1886 with the maturation of area agriculture). By 1886 Nicodemus had two newspapers, a bank, a drugstore, a schoolhouse, three churches, three general stores in the NPS brochure's count, small hotels, an ice cream parlor, and a livery (BlackPast; NPS park brochure; Trust for Public Land). The town billed itself in promotion as "the Largest Colored Colony in America" (as carried in town history accounts).

The August 1 Emancipation celebration was the peak made visible: on August 1, 1886, a reported four to five thousand people descended on Nicodemus for the barbecue and watermelon feast, with visitors from across Kansas and from Oklahoma and Missouri (community history document hosted by the Nicodemus Historical Society; the figure is reported, not independently verified, and is flagged in Section 9).

Population at the peak is the dossier's least settled number, because the counts mix townsite, township, and county and disagree with each other (conflicts 4 and 5). The February 1880 Kansas census counted 700 Black residents in Graham County, about 20 percent of the county; the June 1880 federal census counted 484 Black residents in the county, 260 of them in Nicodemus Township (Erbschloe chronology, noting the two reports conflict). A standard encyclopedia gives 258 Black and 58 white residents in the town and surrounding township by 1880, and about 600 residents in the farming community in the first decade of the 1900s (Encyclopedia.com; Contemporary Black Biography entry). The University of Kansas Libraries summary reports the population peaked in 1910 at 595 by the federal census; a park guide instead gives 400 in 1910 (KU Libraries; parkrangerjohn park guide). The dossier therefore carries peak population as NOT ESTABLISHED rather than choosing among incompatible geographies.

Veryl Switzer belongs to the town's later fame but his roots are in the peak-era farm families: born in Nicodemus in 1932, he became an All-American halfback at Kansas State and was picked fourth overall by the Green Bay Packers in the 1954 NFL draft, played two seasons, served in the Air Force, and returned to farm the land his family had held for generations (NPS-derived family guide).

4. The Turning Point

The turning point was a railroad decision, and it was about money, not a single act of exclusion, which makes it more instructive, not less. Lasting prosperity on the Great Plains depended on a rail line (NPS park brochure). Nicodemus campaigned hard: the town voted $16,000 in bonds to attract a line, and courted three companies, the Missouri Pacific, the Union Pacific, and the Santa Fe (NPS-derived family guide; Wikipedia history summary). The effort failed on terms. A community history account puts it plainly: the Missouri Pacific did not come because the people could not raise the necessary funds to grade the difficult terrain the town sat on (community history document). In 1888 the Union Pacific built its line through Bogue, several miles south of Nicodemus (University of Kansas Libraries; NPS park brochure: "the nearest railroad ran several miles south").

The damage compounded earlier blows. An 1885 blizzard had destroyed 40 percent of the wheat crop and prompted a first exodus from the area (Encyclopedia.com). Poor harvests through the 1880s had already thinned the townsite; one history notes that by 1884 less than 50 people remained in the town itself, though the surrounding farm families persisted and the town's booster campaign of 1886 revived the townsite (Wikipedia history summary). After 1888, businesses moved toward the rail towns and the townsite population drained away, while Black farm families stayed on the surrounding township land, so Nicodemus became a community of primarily Black farm families centered on a shrinking townsite (NPS). At the end of the decade, Oklahoma Territory opened as a competing draw and pulled prospective Black homesteaders away from Kansas (Encyclopedia.com).

5. Survival, Decline, or Destruction

Nicodemus declined for a century and was never destroyed, and the record of the decline is specific:

  • 1910: the federal census figure reported for the area is 595 (KU Libraries); the townsite itself was by then a small village inside a farming township (see conflict 4).
  • 1930s: farm prices fell in the Great Depression and a three-year drought culminated in the Kansas Dust Bowl of 1935; the town's population dropped to 76 (Contemporary Black Biography encyclopedia entry).
  • 1939: Nicodemus Township Hall is built of locally quarried limestone with Works Progress Administration help, serving as community center and seat of local government (HistoryNet).
  • 1953: the post office, established in 1878, is discontinued on an economic decision from Washington (community history document; KU Libraries gives the same closure year, 1953). By 1950 only 16 residents were counted in the town (KU Libraries).
  • 1970s: a descendant-led revitalization begins: donations from former residents fund repairs, and the town gains low-income housing units, a 100-foot water tower, and paved streets, remaking Nicodemus partly as a retirement homecoming place for former residents (community history document).
  • 1976: the original 161-acre townsite is designated a National Historic Landmark district (NPS park brochure).
  • 1988: Angela Bates, a descendant of the 1877 settlers, founds the Nicodemus Historical Society and begins collecting photographs and documents from descendants nationwide, later placing the collection with the University of Kansas Spencer Research Library (Bates's own accounts: Daily Yonder; Humanities Kansas; the founding year is disputed, see conflict 9). Bates had been handed what she calls the preservation baton by her cousin Veryl Switzer (Daily Yonder).
  • 1996: after six years of work by Bates, the Historical Society, the community, and Senators Robert Dole and Pat Roberts with the National Park Service, Congress establishes Nicodemus National Historic Site in November 1996 (NPS; Northeastern University Impact Engines profile of the Historical Society). The site preserves five structures: the St. Francis Hotel, the African Methodist Episcopal Church, the First Baptist Church, the Nicodemus School District No. 1 building, and Nicodemus Township Hall (NPS).
  • 2024: Bates retires after decades as the Historical Society's founding president and executive director (Great Bend Tribune).

6. The Town Today

A visitor today finds a living community of 14 residents in the CDP (2020 census), descendants of the original settlers among them (NPS; Wikipedia census citation), inside a township of 43 (2020 census). The National Park Service and the residents work together to preserve the five historic structures (NPS park brochure), which NPS interpretation maps to the town's five pillars: the A. M. E. Church (religion), the St. Francis Hotel and Switzer Residence (commerce), School District No. 1 (education), Township Hall (community), and the First Baptist Church (family) (NPS interpretation, summarized in site accounts). The visitor center operates from Township Hall (HistoryNet; Trust for Public Land). The five buildings, as dated by NPS and site histories:

  • St. Francis Hotel / Fletcher-Switzer House: the Fletcher family building that also housed the first post office, the first school, and a stagecoach stop; NPS brochure dates it 1878, building histories date its surviving two-story portion to 1881 (conflict 6).
  • African Methodist Episcopal Church: congregation worshipping from 1878; present building acquired in 1897 from the Mount Pleasant Baptist Church (HistoryNet; NPS brochure date 1897; conflict 8).
  • First Baptist Church: limestone church built 1907, for a congregation that began in a dugout in 1877 (HistoryNet; NPS).
  • Nicodemus School District No. 1 building: built 1918, replacing the 1887 freestanding schoolhouse (HistoryNet; NPS).
  • Nicodemus Township Hall: built 1939, limestone, WPA-assisted; now the visitor center (HistoryNet; NPS).

The keeping organizations: the Nicodemus Historical Society (museum and archives; collection at the University of Kansas Spencer Research Library, https://digital.lib.ku.edu/ku-nicodemus/root), the National Park Service (https://home.nps.gov/places/nicodemus-national-historic-site.htm, park phone (785) 839-4233 per the project's outreach plan), and the township's own residents and descendants. The anchor event is the Emancipation and Homecoming Celebration, held the last weekend of July every year since 1878 by the community's account (project outreach plan; HistoryNet), drawing descendants and former residents from across the country; the 2027 celebration will be billed as the 149th annual (project outreach plan). Contact points from the project record: Nicodemus Historical Society, https://www.nicodemushistoricalsociety.org, (785) 839-4280; NPS site, 304 Washington Avenue, Nicodemus, Kansas.

Song annotation, claim by claim

Annotation of the song itself, claim by claim against the dossier spine:

  • The hook ("From the plains to the page, we rose like dawn"; "Built dreams from dirt, no maps, just song"; "Nicodemus lives where the brave belong"): framing, not a dated claim. "Built dreams from dirt" matches the material record of dugout and sod construction (Section 1); "lives" is literally true, since the community is still inhabited (Sections 5 and 6).
  • "Packed a promise in a wagon, left Jim Crow's net": the migration premise. The Town Company recruited in Kentucky and Tennessee as Reconstruction collapsed, promising land, the vote, and self-government (Section 1). Supported.
  • "Six souls and a scroll, Homestead dreams unfold": the founding count and the land framework in one line. "Homestead" is documented: settlers proved up 160-acre claims around the platted townsite under the Homestead Act of 1862 (Section 1). "Six souls" matches the six Black founders named in the standard account carried in the Key Facts Box (Smith, Carr, Allsap, Roundtree, Lenze, Edmonds) and the user's own ChatGPT town-list entry ("six Black entrepreneurs and a white developer"). The dossier's conflict 1 stands behind the number: NPS counts Smith, five other Black men, and Hill (seven), and a Library of Congress-derived document counts six Black and two white incorporators (eight). The line also passes over W. R. Hill, the company's white treasurer, entirely. Carried as the best-supported count, flagged as disputed.
  • "On Solomon's breeze, we struck Black gold": the townsite sits in the Solomon Valley on the Solomon River (Header; Section 1). Supported.
  • "No bricks, just guts," followed by "sod homes and faith": the September 1877 party found no houses at all, wintered in dugouts, and built in sod before limestone (Sections 1 and 2). Supported.
  • "Prayin' with the Osage, firewood grace": FLAGGED, unverified. No source in this dossier's record mentions the Osage in connection with the colony; the documented outside help in the hard first winter is the relief-supply effort of 1878-79, shipped free by the Kansas Pacific railroad (Section 1). The line echoes the Osage reference in the user's ChatGPT draft lineage ("Gold From the Dust" and its predecessor), so its source is the draft tradition, not a source this dossier can cite. It stays in the song as the user's art; the dossier does not carry it as fact.
  • "Kentucky kin crossed line for a new birth zone" and the closing "From Lexington to legend": the recruited settlers came from Lexington, Kentucky, with further recruitment in Tennessee (Section 1). Supported.
  • "Dugouts held hope, school bells rang": dugouts documented (Section 1); schooling began in 1878 in the Fletcher building with Jenny Smith Fletcher as first teacher, and a freestanding schoolhouse followed in 1887 (Section 2). Supported.
  • "Ministers spit truth while the cattlemen sang": color, anchored in fact. The founding circle was minister-heavy (Smith, Roundtree, and the Reverend Daniel Hickman, who arrived in 1878), and worship began in a dugout in 1877 (Sections 1 and 2). No dossier claim turns on the cattlemen.
  • "Two churches rise like twin towers of light": by 1881 the town held two churches among its roughly 35 structures (Section 2 chronology), the First Baptist congregation (worship from a dugout in 1877) and the A. M. E. congregation (from 1878). Supported for the founding era.
  • "Ballots in the booth" and "we flipped Graham County": voting is the dossier's strongest civic thread: county organization in 1880 with Black residents winning the county clerk's office and township posts, and Edwin P. McCabe's election as state auditor in 1882 (Section 2). "Flipped Graham County" is a compression: the record shows specific offices won inside a majority-white county, not a county takeover, and Nicodemus lost the 1880 county seat vote to Millbrook. The boast points at something real and overstates its size; flagged as compression.
  • "No train? No problem," with "roots still ran deep": the railroad bypass is the dossier's turning point (Section 4): three companies courted, $16,000 in bonds voted, and the Union Pacific line built through Bogue in 1888, several miles south. The community did persist on its township farm base, so the line's second half holds; the shrug in the first half is bravado, since the bypass drove a century of townsite decline (Sections 4 and 5). Flagged as tone, not error.
  • "Depression hit hard, Dust Bowl threw shade": documented (Section 5): farm prices fell in the Great Depression and a three-year drought culminated in the Kansas Dust Bowl of 1935; the township population dropped to 76 in that decade. Supported.
  • "But we held homecomings like parades of brave" and "every summer we sing, every name we say": the Emancipation and Homecoming Celebration, held since 1878 and now the last weekend of July, is the town's anchor event and its descendant roll call (Sections 2 and 6). Supported.

Conflicts between sources, recorded not smoothed

  1. Town Company membership. NPS brochure: W. H. Smith, five other African American men, and W. R. Hill (seven men). A Library of Congress-derived teaching document: the company was incorporated by six Black and two white Kansans (eight). BlackPast names six Black founders from Lexington, Kentucky, and treats Hill separately as co-promoter. Kansas City Public Library says Smith and Hill worked with five Black ministers and calls it the "Nicodemus Township Company." The dossier carries Smith (president) and Hill (treasurer) plus five other Black founders as the best-supported core and flags the count.
  2. The name's origin. BlackPast and Encyclopedia.com: named for a legendary African slave prince who purchased his freedom. Wikipedia's community summary: named for the Biblical figure Nicodemus. Angela Bates holds both, adding the plantation "Wake Nicodemus" song tradition (Kansas City Public Library). The dossier carries the slave-prince tradition as the founders' stated reason and records the biblical derivation alongside it.
  3. First arrivals. Town histories: Roundtree first, June 18, 1877; the Fletchers in July 1877. Encyclopedia.com: the first thirty colonists arrived July 1877, then 150 in March 1878. NPS brochure: some 300 settlers arrived September 1877. The counts cannot all describe the same party; the dossier carries the sequence (advance settlers summer 1877, main party September 1877, more in spring 1878) and flags the numbers.
  4. Peak population figure. KU Libraries: peaked 1910 at 595 (federal census, as reported there). A park guide: 400 in 1910. Contemporary Black Biography entry: about 600 in the first decade of the 1900s. The geographies differ (townsite versus township) and the sources do not reconcile. Carried as NOT ESTABLISHED.
  5. The 1880 counts. Erbschloe chronology itself records two conflicting census reports: the February 1880 Kansas census counted 700 Black residents in Graham County; the June 1880 federal census counted 484 in the county, 260 in Nicodemus Township. Encyclopedia.com gives 258 Black and 58 white in town and township. All are carried; none is presented as the single truth.
  6. St. Francis Hotel date. NPS brochure lists the St. Francis Hotel as (1878). Building histories date the surviving two-story limestone portion to 1881 (HistoryNet; local building lists). Encyclopedia.com says Fletcher opened the St. Francis Hotel in 1885. The 1878 date likely marks the Fletcher building's first use (post office and school); the dossier carries all three with their sources.
  7. Emancipation celebration founding year. The community's account, Bates's telling, and the project outreach plan all give 1878, the settlers' first full summer, with the first observance on August 1, 1878 (CSJ Kansas; HistoryNet: annual since 1878). The Erbschloe chronology places its "first observed" line in an 1881 sequence, ambiguously. The dossier carries 1878 and flags the ambiguity.
  8. A. M. E. Church date. NPS brochure: (1897). HistoryNet: the congregation worshipped at another site from 1878 and acquired the present building in 1897 from Mount Pleasant Baptist. A local building list gives 1885, possibly a congregation milestone rather than the building. The dossier carries congregation from 1878, present building 1897, and flags 1885.
  9. Historical Society founding year. Bates's own accounts: 1988 (Daily Yonder: "I started the historical society in 1988"; Humanities Kansas: founder, 1988). Northeastern University profile: founded 1989 (possibly an incorporation or formalization date). Great Bend Tribune: founded 1986. The dossier carries 1988 on the founder's own word and flags the others.
  10. National Historic Site date. NPS: Congress established the site in November 1996; the park place page says the legislation was enacted in November 1996. Several park sources give November 12, 1996. The dossier carries November 1996, with November 12 noted as the commonly given day.
  11. Population today. 2020 census: CDP 14; township 43 (both via census-citing summaries). One park guide gives "25 in 2020," which matches neither census unit and appears to be an error or an informal area count. A 2014 account says "just a dozen residents." The dossier carries the CDP figure of 14 (2020) in the Key Facts Box and notes the township figure.
