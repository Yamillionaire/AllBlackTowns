Key Facts Box

  • Founded: 1877. The Nicodemus Town Company was organized in April 1877; the first settler is reported to have arrived June 18, 1877, and the main recruited party from Kentucky arrived in September 1877 (NPS park brochure; BlackPast; town histories summarized in Section 9).
  • Founded by: the Nicodemus Town Company. The members named most often are the Reverend W. H. Smith, a Black minister (company president), and W. R. Hill, a white land developer (company treasurer), together with five other Black men from Lexington, Kentucky; the named six Black founders in one standard account are W. H. Smith, Benjamin Carr, Jerry Allsap, the Reverend Simon P. Roundtree, Jeff Lenze, and William Edmonds (NPS park brochure; BlackPast). The exact membership count is disputed; see conflict 1 in Section 9.
  • Incorporated: not applicable. Nicodemus was never incorporated; it has always been an unincorporated townsite governed through Nicodemus Township (Wikipedia community summary; Graham County township structure).
  • Peak population: NOT ESTABLISHED as a single agreed figure. Reported figures conflict by geography and source: about 600 residents in the town and farming community in the first decade of the 1900s (Contemporary Black Biography encyclopedia entry); 595 in the federal census figure reported for the area in 1910 (University of Kansas Libraries collection summary); about 700 in the mid-1880s (NPS-derived family guide); nearly 1,000 at the high end (Trust for Public Land). The 1880 counts alone disagree (see conflict 5). Do not publish one number until the townsite-versus-township basis is settled.
  • Population today: 14 (Nicodemus CDP, 2020 federal census, via Wikipedia's census citation). Nicodemus Township, which includes the surrounding farm families, counted 43 in the 2020 census (Wikipedia township summary). The project's outreach plan carries "about 14 to 25 residents," consistent with the CDP figure plus nearby households.
  • Known for: the oldest and only remaining Black town west of the Mississippi River founded by formerly enslaved settlers in the Reconstruction era; an Exoduster colony that predates and helped inspire the 1879 Exoduster migration; the Emancipation and Homecoming Celebration held since 1878; a National Historic Site preserving five historic buildings.
  • The song: "Nicodemus, Kansas" (see Section 7).

Introduction

  • Town name, State: Nicodemus, Kansas (site and song usage: "Nicodemus, Kansas")
  • County: Graham County
  • Status: still inhabited, unincorporated community and census-designated place (CDP) in northwestern Kansas, on the Solomon River. Never incorporated as a municipality; civil government is Nicodemus Township. The townsite is the Nicodemus National Historic Site (National Park Service, established 1996) and a National Historic Landmark district (1976).

1. Founding

Nicodemus was a planned colony, not an accident of migration. As Reconstruction collapsed in the South, a group of Black men from Lexington, Kentucky, together with a white land promoter, organized the Nicodemus Town Company in April 1877 to found an all-Black town on the Kansas plains, where Black settlers could own land, vote, and govern themselves (NPS; BlackPast). W. H. Smith, a Black minister who had already homesteaded in the area, served as company president; W. R. Hill, a white land developer from Indiana, served as treasurer (town histories via Legends of America; Kansas City Public Library). The company platted a townsite in the Solomon Valley of Graham County, in northwestern Kansas, and recruited settlers in Kentucky and Tennessee with circulars and church visits; the Reverend Simon P. Roundtree recruited in Kentucky, and Benjamin "Pap" Singleton, the Nashville organizer later called the Moses of the Colored Exodus, also distributed the company's circulars (Legends of America, summarizing the standard histories).

The name carried the founding argument. The founders named the town for Nicodemus, remembered in Black tradition as a legendary African slave prince who purchased his freedom (BlackPast; Encyclopedia.com), and Angela Bates, the town historian and a descendant, adds the biblical Nicodemus and the plantation song line "Wake Nicodemus" for the day of Jubilee as the name's other roots (Kansas City Public Library). Both derivations circulate; see conflict 2.

Settlement came in stages, and the sources do not agree on the numbers (conflict 3). Roundtree is reported as the first settler, arriving June 18, 1877 (town histories). Zachary T. Fletcher and his wife Jenny Smith Fletcher, the Reverend Smith's daughter, arrived in July 1877; Fletcher became company secretary, then the town's first postmaster and its first entrepreneur (BlackPast; town histories). One encyclopedia account counts the first thirty colonists in July 1877 and 150 more in March 1878 (Encyclopedia.com). The National Park Service brochure gives the main arrival as some 300 settlers recruited from Kentucky in September 1877, and records that sixty of them, shaken by the dugout dwellings, returned to Kentucky immediately (NPS park brochure).

What the September party found was a plat on paper and a prairie in fact: no houses, no trees, no shade, only tall grass (NPS-derived family guide). Families wintered in dugouts, pits dug into the earth and roofed over, along the Solomon River draws. Williana Hickman, who arrived with her husband the Reverend Daniel Hickman in the spring of 1878, remembered being told the smoke rising from the ground was Nicodemus, and crying at the sight (her later account, quoted in town histories). Settlers took up homestead claims of 160 acres around the townsite under the Homestead Act of 1862 while the company sold town lots; the legal title to the townsite itself was later challenged by land speculator Henry Miller, and the town received its official title on June 6, 1886 (Wikipedia history summary; flagged in Section 9 as needing a primary-source check).

The first winter nearly broke the colony. Through the winter of 1878-79 the settlers depended in part on relief supplies solicited by agents such as the Reverend Silas M. Lee, shipped free by the Kansas Pacific railroad and issued from a commissary by town officers; in April 1879 the Nicodemus Town Company was abolished and each man left to, in the period phrase, hoe his own row (Kansas Historical Society digital collections, historical article on the colony). The town survived the aid winter, and its timing mattered: Nicodemus was founded in 1877, two years before the great Exoduster migration of 1879, and its circulars and example helped draw that later wave toward Kansas (Kansas Historical Society, Kansapedia; Kansas City Public Library).
